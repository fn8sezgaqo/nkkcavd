Medical history review – chronic conditions, surgeries, and medications

Hormone level testing – estrogen, testosterone, and thyroid panels

Physical and pelvic exam – to rule out physiological causes

Emotional and relationship assessment – stress, mood, and intimacy concerns

Lifestyle evaluation – sleep, exercise, and substance use

Low Libido in Women: What Doctors Look at First

Low libido in women is a common concern, affecting up to 40% of adult women at some point. While it can feel distressing, understanding what your doctor will assess first can help you know what to expect—and how to prepare for a productive visit.

1. Defining Low Libido in Women

"Low libido" means a reduced interest in sexual activity. It isn't just about frequency: it's about desire, arousal, and satisfaction. In women, low libido can show up as:

Feeling uninterested in sex most of the time

Difficulty becoming aroused or staying aroused

Decreased sexual thoughts or fantasies

Avoidance of intimacy due to lack of desire

Because sexual desire varies widely, doctors focus on changes from your personal baseline—what's normal for you.

2. Why Your Doctor Takes a Holistic View

Sexual health is shaped by physical, emotional, relationship, and lifestyle factors. Rather than jumping to conclusions, your doctor will start with a broad assessment to find potential causes and rule out serious conditions.

3. Medical History and Symptom Timeline

Your first appointment often centers on questions about:

Onset & DurationWhen did you first notice decreased desire? Was it sudden (weeks) or gradual (months to years)?

Life EventsPregnancy, childbirth, menopause transition, illness, surgery, or major stressors can trigger changes.

Pattern of SymptomsAre symptoms constant or do they come and go? Any links to your menstrual cycle, mood shifts, or medications?

Other Sexual ConcernsPain during intercourse, difficulty reaching orgasm, or vaginal dryness.

Documenting a clear timeline helps your doctor narrow down potential triggers.

4. Medication Review

Many prescriptions and over-the-counter drugs can dampen libido. Your physician will review:

Antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs)

Blood Pressure Medications (beta-blockers, diuretics)

Hormonal Contraceptives (birth control pills, patches, IUDs)

Pain Medications (opioids)

Allergy/Cold Remedies (first-generation antihistamines)

Bringing a list—or bottles—of everything you take (including vitamins and supplements) avoids surprises.

5. Hormonal Evaluation

Sex drive in women is influenced by hormones. Your doctor may order blood tests for:

Estrogen – Low levels (especially around menopause) can reduce desire and cause vaginal dryness.

Testosterone – Though present at low levels in women, it plays a key role in arousal.

Thyroid Function – Both underactive and overactive thyroids can affect libido.

Prolactin – Elevated levels (from pituitary issues or certain medications) can suppress sex drive.

Test results guide targeted treatments, from hormone replacement to medication adjustments.

6. Physical and Pelvic Exam

A basic physical exam helps rule out medical causes:

General Health CheckBlood pressure, weight, and overall wellness.

Pelvic ExamTo assess vaginal health, atrophy, or signs of infection.

7. Psychological and Emotional Factors

Mental health has a huge impact on libido. Doctors will explore:

Stress & AnxietyWork pressure, financial worries, caregiving responsibilities.

DepressionA major factor in diminished sexual interest and pleasure.

Body Image & Self-EsteemHow you feel about your appearance can influence desire.

Past TraumaHistory of sexual abuse or trauma can affect current sexual experiences.

Honest conversation helps your doctor identify if therapy, counseling, or stress-reduction techniques might help.

8. Relationship Dynamics

A supportive, communicative relationship is key to a healthy sex life. Your doctor may ask:

How satisfied are you with emotional closeness?

Are there unresolved conflicts or trust issues?

Do you and your partner share expectations about frequency and type of sexual activity?

Couples counseling or sex therapy can be recommended if relationship factors play a major role.

9. Lifestyle and Habits

Doctors will review everyday habits that influence sexual desire:

Sleep QualityChronic poor sleep can lower sex drive.

ExerciseBoth too little and too much intense exercise can disrupt hormones.

Nutrition & HydrationBalanced diet supports energy levels and hormone health.

Alcohol & Substance UseExcessive alcohol or recreational drugs can impair arousal and performance.

Small lifestyle shifts—like a regular sleep schedule or a balanced diet—often boost libido.

10. When to Consider Further Testing

If initial assessments don't reveal clear causes, your doctor might suggest:

Ultrasound (to check reproductive organs)

Referral to an Endocrinologist (for complex hormone issues)

Referral to a Mental Health Professional (for in-depth therapy)

Sex Therapist Consultation

These specialists collaborate to create a personalized treatment plan.
