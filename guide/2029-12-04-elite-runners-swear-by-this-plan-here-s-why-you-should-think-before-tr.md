# Elite Runners Swear By This Plan. Here's Why You Should Think Before Trying Double Threshold Training.

SCROLL THROUGH RUNNING TikTok or watch enough YouTube workout videos and you’ll start seeing the phrase “double threshold” pop up. It’s a training method associated with seemingly superhuman elite athletes: Norwegian Jakob Ingebrigtsen, the Olympic Champion in the 1500 meters and 5000 meters; Australian record holder in the mile Cameron Meyers; U.S. 10K champion Emily Infeld. Basically, double threshold is the running world's super-targeted version of a two-a-day plan.

The methodology isn't new, though. This kind of training has been around since the 1970s, and was popularized in the 1980s by legendary running coach Jack Daniels. But, more recently, the Norwegians—especially Ingebrigtsen—have turned it into content. And because everyone looks to the top to see what’s working, you’ve probably seen runners add “double threshold day” to their Strava captions any time they log a second session in a single day.

But what exactly does it mean to implement a double-threshold training plan, and should you be doing it? Short answer: probably not. It’s nuanced and highly dependent on understanding effort—the type of plan most runners shouldn’t touch until they’ve built years of consistent training and a high weekly mileage base. Here’s what you need to know.

What Is Double Threshold Training?

First, you need to understand threshold training in the general sense before doubling up. Developing your cardiovascular fitness is “kind of like building a house,” explains Zac Marion, a doctor of physical therapy and running coach in Denver. “Long, easy runs help you lay down a foundation, and speed work helps raise the ceiling. Threshold workouts help you build out the walls.”

In plain English: Threshold runs help you run faster, longer, and more efficiently by training your body to clear lactate—a metabolic byproduct that leads to fatigue—more effectively.

[Plan Here's Why You](https://matrix-skin.xianfengchem.com/blog/6259443298.html)

Threshold runs are done at threshold pace, the fastest speed you can maintain before lactate accumulates in the blood at a rate you can’t clear fast enough, which generally happens somewhere between 40 minutes and an hour. There are blood tests that help athletes determine their exact lactate threshold, but they’re not necessary for amateur runners. Instead, think of threshold pace as “comfortably hard,” or a seven on an effort scale of one to 10, says Zach Prescott, a Division I collegiate distance coach at American University. “I ask my college athletes to recite their phone number—if they can’t get it out cleanly, they’re working too hard.”

For most runners, a classic threshold workout might look like six to eight mile or kilometer reps or four to six reps of 10 minutes at threshold pace with 60 to 90 seconds of rest in between.

Double threshold training, then, is exactly what it sounds like: You split one big threshold workout into two separate sessions in the same day—one in the morning, one in the afternoon—with at least six hours between them. So instead of the workouts above, you might do six reps of one mile at threshold pace in the morning and four reps of one mile in the afternoon, or three reps of 12 minutes in the morning, then repeat that in the afternoon.

There are two big benefits to splitting up a threshold workout. First, “you get more time at the right physiological stress level and avoid the point where cumulative fatigue forces you into failure,” Marion says. In a single workout, you might spend 40 to 60 minutes at threshold pace; increasing that and maintaining quality can be tough. With two workouts, you can easily clock 60-plus minutes at threshold pace, and you get recovery time in between, he adds.

“Double threshold workouts also allow you to stack volume more effectively,” says Prescott. Two sessions means two warmups and two cooldowns plus the actual efforts—so suddenly you’re looking at an 18- to 20-mile day that won’t overtax your body the same way a single long run might.

Who Should Do Double Threshold Training?

Double threshold works for elites because they're elite. These athletes already have enormous mileage bases they've developed over years of training, plus the time and resources dedicated to rest, sleep, and fuel between runs since its their actual job. Recreational runners? Not as likely.

[Elite Runners Swear By](https://satietybrief.china-akan.com/)

To be a legitimate candidate for double-threshold training, you need to be operating at an unusually high level of consistency, volume, and durability. “If you’re not already logging 50 to 60 miles a week, you aren’t there yet,” says Marion. Your body simply hasn’t built the base required to absorb the cumulative fatigue of two threshold sessions in a day.

Years of structured training matter, too. “I’m a big believer in stacking training, build over build, year over year,” says Prescott. If you’re newer to running, that means your body hasn’t spent much time adapting to a regular, progressive workload—and you probably lack the long-term durability this method demands.

Even for experienced runners, double threshold workouts only make sense once you’ve maxed out what single threshold sessions can offer. “If you’re doing a six by one-mile session and by the fifth mile you feel cooked and you can tell you’re not staying in the threshold zone, that’s an indicator that splitting the session may be better for you,” says Prescott.

Just as important: You can’t be obsessed with pace. If you go into each session trying to “run fast,” you’re missing the point. “This type of training really forces you to focus on the internal physiological processes, which is why it’s so dependent on effort,” says Marion. Pace will change depending on external factors, which is why “even at the highest levels, these guys are slowing down in the afternoon,” says Prescott. “But they can still get a great physiological effect out of that effort.”

Anyone interested in trying double threshold workouts also needs to be able to fit that type of training into their lifestyle. It requires at least six hours of recovery between sessions, says Marion—and doesn’t mean rushing from a morning workout to a stressful workday on your feet and then trying to hammer out another hard run at night. Without proper recovery, this method can do more harm than good. “The most obvious downside is overtraining,” says Prescott. “And that’s something that can lead to injury and burnout.”

What Types of Running Workouts You Should Do Instead

[Trying Double Threshold Training](https://copperglow.dns0663.com/blog/6734254280.html)

If the physiology behind double threshold training sounds intriguing, there’s good news: You can reap all the benefits of threshold work without ever doubling up. Prescott and Marion agree that most men are far better served by keeping things simple and doing one high-quality threshold session per week.

From there, the real performance gains come from gradually building overall mileage and staying consistent. “If you can execute your weekly threshold session well and pair it with a strong long run and a true speed day, you’ll get far more out of your training than stacking everything on one day just to impress Strava,” says Prescott.

You don’t want to empty the tank so completely on a Tuesday that Friday’s workout becomes a half-hearted effort. The best training is built on balanced, repeatable sessions that allow you to still tackle the next workout—and that requires really listening to your body and understanding what different efforts feel like to you, an instinct you’ll develop over time.

If you’re dead-set on following a two-a-day workout structure, you can also do that without following the threshold format. This might be especially useful if you’re looking to add a strength component to your run training, or if you’re using a session to accumulate easy miles to hit a volume goal. You can do this without the level of experience needed for threshold training, but beginners should still hold off until they’ve established a strong base. And no matter your experience level, make sure you warm up properly and prioritize rest as you would for other sessions.

Double-threshold training sounds like a hack, and—if you look to the elites—the type of workout that could unlock your next PR. But unless you’re already running 50-plus miles a week with years of training under your belt and possess a deep understanding of what the appropriate effort feels like and the time to prioritize recovery like it’s your job, you likely don’t need to double up. You can get there—but you'll need to check those boxes first. In the meantime, keep showing up and training consistently, and you’ll be able to build up for your goals without burning out.
