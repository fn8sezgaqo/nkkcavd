# A Guide to Anti-VEGF Treatments for Wet AMD

The Consumer’s Guide to Anti-VEGF Treatments for Wet AMD

Anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) medications have been a mainstay of treatment for wet age-related macular degeneration (wet AMD) for 20 years — and for good reason. The medication has dramatically improved outcomes for people with the eye condition.

“Before we had anti-VEGF treatments, we [used] surgery, laser, and other medications to treat wet AMD, and none of those treatments actually improved the patient’s vision,” says Zhiyong Yang, MD, PhD, a professor of ophthalmology at McGovern Medical School at University of Texas Health Houston. “We never saw any improvement before we started to use anti-VEGF.”

Today, anti-VEGF medications can stabilize vision for as many as 9 out of 10 people who have wet AMD — and other retina diseases — according to the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO). The drugs can even improve vision in about 1 in 3 people.

7 Essential Facts About Anti-VEGF Treatment for Wet AMD

1. Anti-VEGF Medications Have Revolutionized the Treatment of Wet AMD

Before anti-VEGF drugs were developed, there were no effective treatments for wet AMD. In fact, as many as two-thirds of people with the condition were expected to become legally blind within two years of developing the eye disease, according to the AAO.

Today, the outlook is different for people with wet AMD, says Sarju Patel, MD, MPH, attending physician at Retinal Consultants Medical Group in Northern California. He notes that many people who receive anti-VEGF treatments can retain their vision.

“It’s very effective. It changed everything,” says Dr. Patel. “And, more importantly, it stabilizes the disease … so that it doesn’t continue to progress.”

2. Several Anti-VEGF Medications Are Used to Treat Wet AMD

The anti-VEGF drugs ophthalmologists prescribe most often to treat wet AMD are:

Ranibizumab (Lucentis)

Aflibercept (Eylea)

Faricimab-svoa (Vabysmo)

Bevacizumab (Avastin)

Ranibizumab, aflibercept, and faricimab-svoa have all been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to treat wet AMD.

Bevacizumab, on the other hand, is a medication that was initially used to treat cancer. It has been prescribed off label to treat wet AMD since 2005, according to the AAO. The original anti-VEGF, pegaptanib (Macugen), was approved in 2004 but is no longer available in the United States.

A study examining the long-term outcomes of patients receiving aflibercept, ranibizumab, and bevacizumab found no significant differences among the anti-VEGF medications. And, in a review of studies investigating the safety and effectiveness of anti-VEGF medications for treating wet AMD, researchers concluded the medications in the class were equally effective.

3. Anti-VEGF Medications Have Been Shown to Be Safe

Early research suggested that people who took anti-VEGF medications had a small increased risk of cardiovascular problems, including heart attack and stroke, but other research — including a review published in Ophthalmology Retina — has found no increase in mortality rates among people who take anti-VEGF medication.

It may be that many of the people affected by wet AMD who had participated in the early studies tended to be older. So, they were already at an increased risk of heart disease, says Patel. Some doctors wait to give anti-VEGF drugs to people who’ve recently had a stroke. But, in general, it doesn’t stop those patients from receiving the treatment later. Even so, more research is needed to determine the long-term effects and safety of blocking VEGF.

Also, women who are pregnant cannot get anti-VEGF treatment.

4. Anti-VEGF Medications Are Injected Directly Into the Eye in a Doctor’s Office

The procedure is simple and only takes around 30 minutes, according to Dr. Yang. First, the ophthalmologist will clean the area around the eye (or eyes) to be treated to prevent infection. They’ll then apply drops to the eye to numb it.

Once the drops take effect, the doctor will insert a very thin needle into the white part of the eye to inject the anti-VEGF medication directly into the jelly-like structure called the vitreous.

After the procedure, it’s important not to touch or rub the eye. The doctor should be called if there are signs of infection, such as pain or redness. Rarely, anti-VEGF medications can also cause inflammation in the eye. Symptoms can include vision loss, light sensitivity, pain, and redness.

5. Anti-VEGF Treatment Is Almost Always a Lifelong Commitment

There is no cure for wet AMD, so most people require ongoing anti-VEGF treatment, says Patel. Clinical trials have shown that more than 90 percent of people treated with anti-VEGF medications keep their vision, according to the AAO.

In the real world, though, only about 50 percent of people who take anti-VEGF medications keep their vision, partly because, unlike clinical trial participants, they don’t receive the injections as often as they should. Older adults sometimes have trouble getting to appointments and may also have other health conditions, both of which may prevent them from getting regular injections.

People who respond well enough to anti-VEGF treatment may be able to go longer than a month or two between injections, which could make it easier to keep up with them. But, “If you stop treatment completely, there’s a very good chance [you] can lose vision,” says Raj Maturi, MD, a clinical spokesperson for the AAO and retina specialist at Midwest Eye Institute in Carmel, Indiana.

For only a fraction of people does the disease “go quiet,” meaning the vision loss doesn’t get worse, says Patel. And, even people who are able to stop getting injections will need to be monitored regularly by an ophthalmologist for signs of a reemergence of wet AMD.

6. The Most Recently Approved Anti-VEGF Drug, Faricimab, Works on Two Proteins

Faricimab differs from other anti-VEGF medications in that it targets not only VEGF proteins, but also angiopoietin-2 proteins, which can cause abnormal blood vessel growth, too, says Patel.

Targeting these two proteins may allow for less frequent shots, but Yang notes that more studies need to be done to prove this. “In theory, it should work better than the medication that only targets VEGF, but this is a relatively new medication,” says Yang. “We need more data.”

7. More Promising Treatments for Wet AMD Are on the Horizon

Although anti-VEGF medications are highly effective, researchers continue to look for and develop better approaches to treating wet AMD, including options that don’t require ongoing injections.

One of these is gene therapy — a onetime shot that can help the eye produce its own anti-VEGF therapy. It could be injected underneath the retina in a surgical procedure or into the eye, according to Dr. Maturi.

Another medication being studied in clinical trials is a VEGF inhibitor called OPT-302, which could also treat wet AMD by targeting the VEGF protein.

Both options look promising and, Maturi says, “There’s a good chance they’ll be approved in the next few years.” For that reason, he always tells his patients about them, partly because he’s seen people who are disheartened when they learn they have to get eye injections for the rest of their lives. “We are coming very quickly to an era where the number of injections needed will be quite limited,” he adds.
