RBC Blood Test: Why Your Count Is Off + Medically Approved Next Steps

An RBC blood test (Red Blood Cell count) is a common lab test that measures how many red blood cells you have in your blood. It's usually part of a Complete Blood Count (CBC) and helps doctors evaluate your overall health.

Red blood cells carry oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. If your RBC blood test results are too high or too low, it can affect how well your organs and tissues function.

Let's break down what your results may mean — and what to do next.

What Is an RBC Blood Test?

An RBC blood test measures the number of red blood cells in a specific volume of blood.

Red blood cells:

Carry oxygen using hemoglobin

Help remove carbon dioxide

Support energy levels and organ function

The test is often ordered:

During routine checkups

If you have fatigue, weakness, or dizziness

Normal RBC Blood Test Ranges

Normal ranges vary slightly by lab, but generally:

Men: ~4.7 to 6.1 million cells per microliter

Women: ~4.2 to 5.4 million cells per microliter

Children: Slightly lower and vary by age

Your doctor will interpret your results based on your age, sex, medical history, and other lab values like hemoglobin and hematocrit.

What Does It Mean If Your RBC Blood Test Is Low?

A low RBC count is commonly associated with anemia.

Common Causes of Low RBC Count

Iron deficiency (most common cause worldwide)

Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency

Chronic kidney disease

Blood loss (heavy periods, ulcers, injury)

Chronic inflammation or autoimmune disease

Bone marrow disorders

Cancer or cancer treatment

Symptoms of Low RBC Count

You may notice:

Fatigue

Weakness

Pale skin

Shortness of breath

Dizziness

Cold hands and feet

Chest discomfort in severe cases

Is a Low RBC Count Dangerous?

Mild anemia is common and often treatable.However, severe anemia can:

Strain the heart

Reduce oxygen to organs

Cause fainting or chest pain

If you experience chest pain, shortness of breath at rest, or fainting, seek medical attention immediately.

What Does It Mean If Your RBC Blood Test Is High?

A high RBC count is called erythrocytosis or polycythemia.

Common Causes of High RBC Count

Dehydration (most common cause)

Smoking

Living at high altitude

Chronic lung disease

Heart disease

Testosterone therapy

Bone marrow disorders (such as polycythemia vera)

Symptoms of High RBC Count

Some people have no symptoms. Others may experience:

Headaches

Blurred vision

High blood pressure

Itching (especially after warm showers)

Blood clots in severe cases

Is a High RBC Count Dangerous?

Mild elevations are often temporary (especially due to dehydration).

However, significantly high levels can:

Increase blood thickness

Raise the risk of blood clots

Increase stroke or heart attack risk

If your RBC blood test is markedly elevated, your doctor may order additional tests to rule out serious causes.

Other Important RBC Blood Test Markers

Your RBC blood test is often interpreted alongside other measurements:

Hemoglobin (Hgb): Amount of oxygen-carrying protein

Hematocrit (Hct): Percentage of blood made up of red cells

MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume): Size of red blood cells

MCH/MCHC: Hemoglobin content per cell

RDW (Red Cell Distribution Width): Variation in cell size

These values help determine:

Whether anemia is due to iron deficiency

If vitamin deficiencies are present

Whether bone marrow disorders may be involved

Medically Approved Next Steps If Your RBC Blood Test Is Off

If your RBC blood test is abnormal, don't panic — but don't ignore it either.

Here's what doctors typically recommend:

1. Repeat the Test

Sometimes abnormal results are due to:

Dehydration

Lab variation

Temporary illness

Your doctor may recheck levels in a few weeks.

2. Check Iron, B12, and Folate Levels

If your RBC count is low, additional blood work may include:

Iron studies

Ferritin

Vitamin B12

Folate

Treatment depends on the cause:

Iron supplements for iron deficiency

B12 injections or oral supplements

Dietary changes

Never start supplements without medical guidance — too much iron can be harmful.

3. Evaluate Kidney and Thyroid Function

Kidney disease can reduce erythropoietin (a hormone that stimulates RBC production).Thyroid disorders can also affect red blood cell levels.

4. Address Underlying Causes

Depending on your results, your doctor may recommend:

Treating heavy menstrual bleeding

Managing chronic diseases

Adjusting medications

Smoking cessation

5. Referral to a Specialist

If a serious condition is suspected, you may be referred to:

A hematologist (blood specialist)

A kidney specialist

An oncologist

This is not automatically a sign of cancer. It simply ensures thorough evaluation.

Lifestyle Steps That Support Healthy RBC Levels

While medical evaluation is key, you can support healthy red blood cell levels by:

Eating iron-rich foods (lean meats, spinach, beans)

Including vitamin C (helps iron absorption)

Getting adequate B12 (meat, dairy, fortified foods)

Staying hydrated

Avoiding smoking

Managing chronic conditions

However, lifestyle changes should complement — not replace — medical evaluation.

When to Speak to a Doctor Immediately

Seek urgent medical care if you experience:

Chest pain

Even if symptoms are mild, you should speak to a doctor about any abnormal RBC blood test result to determine whether further testing is needed. Some causes are simple and easily treated. Others may require prompt intervention.

The Bottom Line

An abnormal RBC blood test is a signal — not a diagnosis.

A low RBC count often points to anemia, commonly due to iron deficiency.

A high RBC count may result from dehydration, smoking, altitude, or rarely, bone marrow disorders.

Many causes are manageable when identified early.

The key is thoughtful follow-up — not fear.
