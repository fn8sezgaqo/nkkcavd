Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis) and golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis) can bench even the most dedicated racquet or club enthusiast. Pain, stiffness and reduced grip strength often keep you off your favorite court or course. Understanding Tennis Elbow and Golfer's Elbow

Tennis Elbow: Inflammation or microtears in the tendons attaching forearm muscles to the outside of the elbow. Common causes include repetitive backhand strokes, heavy gripping and overuse.

Golfer's Elbow: Similar tendon damage on the inside of the elbow. Triggered by repetitive wrist flexion, gripping golf clubs or other activities like hammering.

Sharp or burning pain on the elbow's outer or inner bony bump

Weak grip strength

Pain worsening with lifting, gripping or twisting motions

Left unmanaged, these conditions can persist for months, impact daily activities and even lead to chronic tendon degeneration.

Evidence Supporting RLT for Elbow Tendinopathy

Multiple clinical studies have evaluated red light therapy for lateral elbow tendinopathy:

Randomized trials report significant pain reduction and improved grip strength compared to placebo treatments.

Meta-analyses show RLT yields moderate-to-large improvements in pain and function after 4–8 weeks of treatment.

Near-infrared wavelengths (around 830 nm) often produce deeper tissue effects, beneficial for tendon healing.

While more large-scale trials are still needed, the current body of research suggests RLT is a safe, effective add-on to standard care.

Practical Protocols: How to Use Red Light Therapy

Whether you visit a sports medicine clinic or use an at-home device, follow these guidelines:

Choose the Right Device

Clinical systems: Higher power, professional settings, precise dosing.

Home units: Handheld or wrap-around LED panels with specified wavelengths (600–700 nm and/or 800–900 nm).

Treatment Parameters

Wavelength: 630–670 nm (red) and/or 800–900 nm (near-infrared).

Power density (irradiance): 20–100 mW/cm² for home units; clinics may use up to 200 mW/cm².

Dose (fluence): 4–10 J/cm² per session is typical.

Frequency and Duration

2–3 sessions per week.

5–10 minutes per session, depending on device power and targeted area size.

Total course: 4–8 weeks, reassessing symptoms and function periodically.

Complementary Strategies

Red light therapy works best as part of a comprehensive rehabilitation plan:

Stretching and Strengthening

Eccentric wrist extensor/flexor exercises help rebuild tendon resilience.

Gentle stretching before and after RLT sessions.

Activity Modification

Adjust grip size on racquets or clubs.

Use vibration-dampening grips or elbow straps to offload tendon stress.

Manual Therapy

Soft tissue massage, myofascial release and joint mobilizations can improve circulation and flexibility.

Orthotics and Supports

Counterforce braces or straps reduce tendon load during activity.

Night splints help maintain a neutral wrist position if nighttime pain is an issue.

Safety and Precautions

Red light therapy is generally safe when used correctly. However:

Avoid direct exposure to eyes—always wear protective goggles if recommended.

Do not use over open wounds, active infections or tumorous lesions without medical supervision.

Check with your healthcare provider if you have photosensitivity disorders or take photosensitizing medications.

Monitoring Progress

Keep a simple pain and function diary to track daily changes.

Measure grip strength or use a visual analog scale (VAS) for pain.

Significant improvement? Continue the plan and gradually return to sport.

Modest or no change? Consult a specialist to adjust your protocol or explore other treatments.

When to Seek Further Evaluation

Severe, unrelenting pain

Numbness, tingling or weakness spreading beyond the elbow
