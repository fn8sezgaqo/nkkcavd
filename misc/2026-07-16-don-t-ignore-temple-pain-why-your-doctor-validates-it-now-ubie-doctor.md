1. Anatomy of the Temple Region

The temples sit on each side of your head, roughly between your eyes and your ears. Key structures include:

Temporal artery: A blood vessel that supplies the scalp.

Temporalis muscle: Helps you chew and move your jaw.

Trigeminal nerve branches (V2, V3): Carry sensation and pain signals.

Fascia and connective tissue: Surround muscles and nerves.

Any irritation, inflammation, or tension in these structures can trigger a temple headache.

4. How Doctors Evaluate Your Symptoms

When you describe a sharp stabbing pain in temple area, your physician will:

Onset, location, duration, intensity, and triggers

Associated features (nausea, visual changes, jaw pain)

Palpate the temples for tenderness or enlarged arteries

Neurological screening: strength, reflexes, sensation

Jaw exam: clicking, range of motion

Blood tests (ESR, CRP) to check for inflammation (temporal arteritis)

Imaging (ultrasound, MRI) to view arteries or rule out structural lesions

5. Self-Care Strategies for Temple Headaches

While your doctor completes any needed evaluations, you can try these measures to ease pain:

Apply a cold or warm compress to the temple area

Practice relaxation techniques: deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation

Maintain good posture at work and during screen time

Use over-the-counter pain relievers (ibuprofen or acetaminophen) as directed

Limit caffeine and stay well-hydrated

Ensure a consistent sleep schedule

6. Red Flags: When to Seek Immediate Care

If you experience any of the following alongside temple pain, call emergency services or go to the nearest ER:

Sudden, severe headache ("thunderclap" pain)

Vision changes: double vision, loss of vision in one or both eyes
