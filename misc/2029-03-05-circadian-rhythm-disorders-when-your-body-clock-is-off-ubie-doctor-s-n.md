Circadian Rhythm Disorders: When Your Body Clock Is Off

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Your circadian rhythm is your body's internal 24-hour clock that regulates sleep, hormone production, eating patterns, and alertness. When this rhythm falls out of sync, you may develop circadian rhythm sleep disorders, including:

Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder (falling asleep and waking up much later than desired)

Advanced sleep-wake phase disorder (falling asleep and waking up much earlier than desired)

Shift work disorder (caused by non-traditional work hours)

Jet lag (from rapid travel across time zones)

Common symptoms include insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness, difficulty concentrating, and mood changes. Treatment options often involve timed light therapy (morning or evening), melatonin supplementation, chronotherapy, and behavioral adjustments to realign your internal clock.

Because circadian rhythm disorders can mimic other sleep, mood, or medical conditions, identifying the underlying cause is essential to getting the right treatment. Explanation

Your body runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle called the circadian rhythm. This internal clock influences sleep and wake times, hormone release, eating habits, and more. When your circadian rhythm is out of sync with your environment, you may experience a circadian rhythm disorder. Understanding these disorders can help you recognize symptoms early and take steps toward better sleep and overall health.

What Is a Circadian Rhythm Disorder?

A circadian rhythm disorder occurs when the timing of your internal clock doesn't match the outside world. Common types include:

Delayed Sleep-Wake Phase Disorder (DSWPD): You fall asleep late and wake up late.

Advanced Sleep-Wake Phase Disorder (ASWPD): You fall asleep early and wake up early.

Non-24-Hour Sleep-Wake Disorder: Your sleep time shifts each day (common in blind individuals).

Shift Work Sleep Disorder: Your work schedule conflicts with natural sleep times.

Jet Lag Disorder: Rapid travel across time zones disrupts your rhythm.

Key Symptoms

Symptoms vary by type but often include:

Difficulty falling asleep or waking up

Excessive daytime sleepiness

Insomnia or fragmented sleep

Reduced alertness or performance at work/school

Mood changes (irritability, low motivation)

Digestive problems or headaches

Causes and Risk Factors

Several factors can throw your body clock off balance:

Genetics: Some people inherit a tendency toward early or late sleep schedules.

Light Exposure: Bright light in the evening or too little daylight can shift your rhythm.

Lifestyle: Irregular sleep times, frequent travel, or rotating shifts.

Health Conditions: Mental health disorders (depression, bipolar), neurodegenerative diseases, or other sleep disorders.

Age: Teens often experience a natural delay in sleep phase; older adults may shift toward an early phase.

Diagnosis

To diagnose a circadian rhythm disorder, a health provider may:

Review your sleep history and daily routines.

Ask you to keep a sleep diary or use a wearable tracker for 1–2 weeks.

Conduct a physical exam and screen for underlying conditions.

Order a sleep study (polysomnography) in cases of suspected co-existing sleep disorders.

Measure melatonin levels or core body temperature in specialized tests.

Melatonin Supplements

Chronotherapy

Gradually shifting bed and wake times by 15–30 minutes each day.

Effective but requires strict adherence.

Behavioral Strategies

Maintain a consistent sleep-wake schedule, even on weekends.

Limit caffeine and alcohol, especially later in the day.

Create a dark, cool, quiet sleep environment.

Avoid screen time 1–2 hours before bed.

Medications

Short-term use of sleep aids may be recommended in certain cases.

Prescription stimulants or wake-promoting agents can help with daytime sleepiness in shift work disorder.

Lifestyle Adjustments

Small daily habits can support a healthier circadian rhythm:

Regular Exercise: Morning workouts help advance rhythms; avoid vigorous exercise close to bedtime.

Meal Timing: Eat meals at consistent times; avoid heavy dinners late at night.

Light Exposure: Get natural sunlight early in the day; dim lights in the evening.

Digital Curfew: Use "night mode" on devices to reduce blue light exposure.

Relaxation Techniques: Meditation, deep breathing, or gentle yoga before bed.

Long-Term Outlook

With proper management, most people realign their sleep patterns and improve daily functioning. Treatment is often ongoing, especially for non-24-hour disorder or permanent shift work. Regular check-ins with a sleep specialist or your primary doctor help adjust your plan as needed.

Tips for Staying on Track

Plan gradual changes over weeks, not days.

Be patient—realignment can take 1–3 months.

Keep a sleep diary to monitor progress.

Share your schedule with family or roommates for support.

Discuss any new or worsening symptoms with your healthcare provider.

If you have concerns about a circadian rhythm disorder or anything that could be life-threatening or serious, speak to a doctor.
