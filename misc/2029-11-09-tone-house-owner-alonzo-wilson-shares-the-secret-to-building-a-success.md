# Tone House Owner Alonzo Wilson Shares the Secret to Building a Successful Company

This story is part of Black in Fitness, a series of articles highlighting the challenges and triumphs faced by Black trainers, athletes, and gym owners within the fitness community. Read the rest of the stories here.

Alonzo Wilson, 45, opened the doors of his gym, Tone House, in April 2014. Now, 10 years later, the athletics-based strength and conditioning facility, which has gained a reputation as one of the toughest workouts around, continues to thrive in NYC. He attributes the studio's success to the strength of the community, and the passion that he puts into every workout. It didn’t come easily, though, as he worked to navigate the fitness business world as a Black man. Here, in his own words, he shares how he built his gym.

[Tone House](https://pepvoice.com/)

MY PASSION FOR fitness began way back when I was 23 years old. My mother had cancer, and she passed away at that time. One thing that stuck with me was when I asked the doctor what work we could’ve done to try to prevent her situation. She was obese. He said that she would have had a better fighting chance had she been in better physical condition. That became the reason I started training people.

I was the trainer for the Harlem Magic Master Entertainment basketball team, and I had about 20 personal training clients around NYC for the first 11 years of my career. I would have clients from 5 a.m. to 9 p.m. every day. Some days, even later.

Eventually, I realized I wanted to build something for people that allows them to train and have a similar support system the way athletes do. When I was in high school, we had about 5,500 students, and maybe 500 of them played a sport: basketball, football, track, lacrosse, etc. But the other 5,000 students didn't necessarily get an opportunity to be on the team or were focused on other things. So all those people missed out on the camaraderie and belonging that come with being on a team. And, a lot of times people see athletes and think, wow, this athlete's in such amazing shape. What they don't realize is [those athletes] have such a huge support system, from dietitians to [personal trainers] to teammate support.

That’s where the idea of Tone House came about: Build a gym not just with strength and conditioning classes, but also yoga, access to a dietician and physical therapy, and cold plunges in the locker rooms. Not only does it support you physically, but the community becomes incredibly strong when you’re training like a team.

Starting a business is going to be hard for anyone. But for a person of color, obviously, that’s going to be magnified. Getting the capital to start an idea that you have is the first [step]. You can have all the ideas you want, but if you don't have any capital, then it's just an idea. One of my personal training clients believed in my idea, and had the capital to help me out. They saw my style of training, and how it was unique, and they felt that would make a profit. They believed in it because they were a product of it.

I hate to say it, but a lot of success has to do with who you know. Business is all about the power of connections. Every client is like a tree with many branches. Do right by your clients, and they will introduce you to their branches. Then those branches become clients, with even more branches. Before you know it, you have a good database of people supporting you. You never know what opportunities those people will provide you.

Even once you get the capital, though, oftentimes you're not taken seriously. I remember going to look at [gym] spaces with my investor, who's a Caucasian gentleman. He would tell the realtors that I was the one running this thing, and yet they would always direct their questions to him. We’d see a space, and the realtor would ask, “Do you think this will work for what you’re doing?” My investor reminded them to ask me, but they would continue presenting the questions to him.

When we first opened Tone House in 2014, group fitness was just starting to be on the up, and there was almost no diversity in the community. You had places like SoulCycle, and those kinds of places didn't have too many African-American people, or any other people of different ethnicities in general. The group fitness industry is getting better though. I can see it. Studios are trying to get a little more diversified with their coaches. But there’s still plenty more that could be done.

Education could be the key to minimizing adversity for people of color looking to open their own gym. Accessible summits and courses on the nuances of finding funding and running a business would be ideal. It would be helpful for potential gym owners to understand who is looking to invest in fitness. Knowledge is power. The more knowledge you have, the better everything becomes.

It's always gonna be a lot harder for an African-American to do anything. But, at the end of the day, I tell people to embrace it. Yes, it's gonna be harder, but that’s alright. Don’t try to pull the "woe is me, I’m the victim" mentality. Remember, the cream always rises to the top. Your business or wherever venture you're trying to do will succeed, as long as it's something that's authentic to you and you’re willing to put the work in for.
