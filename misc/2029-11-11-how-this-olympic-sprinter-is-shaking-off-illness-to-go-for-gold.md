# How This Olympic Sprinter Is Shaking Off Illness to Go for Gold

This story is part two of Men's Health's "Road to the Olympics" series, where six athletes share their training journeys as they prepare to compete at the 2024 Paris Olympics in July. Read all of the athletes' entries here.

Rai Benjamin has a one-track mind. After a fraction of a second separated him from the gold medal in the 400-meter hurdles at the Tokyo 2020 Summer Olympics, he broke down into tears on international television. In preparation for the Paris 2024 Summer Olympics, the 26-year-old has to make life decisions most of us wouldn't give a second thought to—everything has an effect on him getting to the Olympics. His thoughts are consumed by race visualizations, and his conversations with himself are centered around motivating himself. Every day, he's choosing to be an Olympian.That choice comes with sacrifices of all types. “I'm fortunate enough just to have this be the focus and have a supportive family that understands that I’m not gonna talk to them every day,” he says. “My family has always known sacrifice. My mom and dad know what it's like to sacrifice and put things aside to be successful.To prep for Paris, he's training with Olympic gold medalists Joanna Hayes and Quincy Watts, as well as his 400-meter relay teammate Michael Norman and Olympic gold medalist Kendall Ellis. Last time we talked with him, he told us about a typical day of training. When we caught up with him in the early weeks of June, he was coming off a rough bout of illness and explained how he's getting back into the swing of things.

IT WAS LATE April, sometime around there. I was getting ready to run the LA Grand Prix. I woke up on a Friday feeling kind of discombobulated. I ended up going to practice anyway, but didn't finish—I was just too just out of it to work out. I got home, and started feeling like the room was spinning. I thought maybe it was because I don't have enough sugar, or I didn't eat enough carbs that day.

I tried eating, hoping that would make it better. But the combo of that and the room spinning just made me nauseous. I spent about two days feeling like that, and it only got worse. I could barely sit up, I was throwing up. I had terrible vertigo. It was scary, not knowing what was happening. I was like, 'do I have a tumor in my brain? What's going on?'

I found out it was a severe ear infection, and it took me out of training for a good week and a half. I tried to keep everything except for training the same—I stuck with my nutrition plan, hydrated as much as possible, got good sleep. Only thing I eased up on was my supplementation, because I wasn't sure of the side effects they'd cause—if they would make me more dizzy or nauseous.

“I’ve been in situations where I’ve gotten hurt preparing for a major championship. When that happens, I have to TRUST IN THE WORK that I’d already done.”

I got medication, and slowly got cleared by a doctor, but it took me a long time to get my body recalibrated back to normal at practice. I'd take two steps and I would veer towards the left or right a little bit. My balance was so off. It was tough, especially knowing I had the LA Grand Prix coming up in just a few weeks. I wouldn't say I was worried, but moreso that I didn't know what to expect. My coaches were pretty adamant about me just hopping back in—so I did, and I was pretty winded even after my warmup. But it only really took me a few days to get back to where I was, and I performed pretty well at the meet. I even ran the fastest time in the world right now—46.64 seconds in the 400m hurdles. It broke the track record at Drake Stadium, and was the ninth-fastest time ever recorded in that event.

Competing like that, knowing what I'd just been through, gave me confidence. It reminded me that I am healthy, and I'm doing what I'm supposed to do. And, to be honest, I surprisingly wasn't super worried about how getting sick would affect my Olympic performance. I've been in situations where I've gotten hurt preparing for a major championship. When that happens, I have to trust in the work that I'd already done. And trust my body to know that I'll be able to get back in shape quickly. And that's exactly what happened.

Now, I'm zoned in on Olympic trials at the end of June. It's kind of funny—your first year going to trials feel so special and you don't know what to expect. But, after years of experience of competing at this high level, you start to feel a sense of calmness going into big meets like this. Not to take it for granted, of course. You always know how special it is to be there. But we always say—the trials are harder than the Olympics. Everyone is just so good. You can't look at it like any other meet. People come out and they do some special things—they'll run faster than they've ever run before. Tension is high. But after years of doing it, you just know you're going to be fine. So I'm feeling good.
