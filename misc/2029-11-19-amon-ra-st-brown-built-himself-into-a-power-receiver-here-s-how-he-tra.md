# Amon-Ra St. Brown Built Himself Into a Power Receiver. Here's How He Trains for NFL Strength.

EVERY DAY AFTER practice, Detroit Lions wide receiver Amon-Ra St. Brown plays catch (and a lot of it) with the Jugs machine. Whirrr… BOOM!…22…23… One-handed. Two-handed. Over the shoulder. The rest of the team, exhausted from hours of route running, tackling drills, strength training, and mobility work, has already trudged toward the showers. Not St. Brown. 156…157…158…

He catches exactly 202 balls, then calls it a day. It’s a drill he’s repeated ever since he was in middle school and his father, two-time Mr. Universe and three-time Mr. World winner John Brown, asked a fellow football dad why the man’s son had such great hands. The dad replied that his son caught 200 balls a day. John told Amon-Ra to catch that—plus two— every single day.

Now 25 years old, St. Brown hasn’t stopped since, meaning he’s piled up tens of thousands of catches. And all those reps have him oozing confidence during a recent interview on the field at his alma mater, Mater Dei High School in Santa Ana, California, before his summer minicamp for kids. “When I’m on the field, I’m not even thinking about catching the ball,” he says. His vibe is chill. To St. Brown, catching the ball truly is easy. When you shake his meaty hand, you realize why, too. “I’ve repped it so many times that it’s second nature. High, low, left, right. Whatever it is, I feel like I’m gonna catch it.”

That’s exactly how a star receiver should feel—especially in an NFL that’s increasingly all about receivers. Yes, quarterbacks are always most likely to be named MVP, and last year, running backs surged back to prominence (Saquon Barkley and Derrick Henry, we’re looking at you). But there’s a reason Netflix launched an entire show last fall called Receiver (which included St. Brown): No player can be as instantly game-changing as an übertalented wideout.

Really, no position epitomizes strength (in all its forms) more than wide receiver. Playing receiver requires plenty of classic physical strength, whether you’re ex–MH cover guy DK Metcalf, who simply bullies defenders into letting him make catches, or you’re Tyreek Hill using speed to rocket past them to snag deep bombs. It also requires next-level mental toughness to take a vicious hit right after making a catch.

“You’re anticipating it,” says St. Brown.

When a player does it right, he becomes a QB’s best friend in the clutch. Over the past four seasons, just 20 players have racked up 30 or more third-down catches in a season. All have been receivers. NFL teams see all this too, which is why they chase franchise WRs. Since 2021, 25 receivers have been chosen in the NFL Draft’s first round, more than twice the total number of tight end and running back picks combined. More and more receivers are making bank as well: Nine wideouts will crack the $30 million average salary threshold this season.

As the electric engine for the league’s highest-scoring team, St. Brown is part of that $30 million club, and he does it with his own unique style. At six feet, 202 pounds, he isn’t the biggest receiver or the fastest (4.51-second 40-yard dash). Yet somehow he has 430 catches in four years as a pro, tied with Hill for tops in the NFL. And he’s devastating when he has the ball: He’s piled up 2,030 yards after the catch in his career, and he moves so much like a running back that the Lions even occasionally give him handoffs (he has 186 career rushing yards).

“You do so much more than catch the ball,” he says. “I love run after catch. Especially playing out in the slot, doing a bunch of different types of things. You might catch a 5-yard slant, but a 5-yard slant could turn to a 20-, 30-, 40-, 50-yard gain, or even a touchdown. You wanna get and make as many explosive plays as you can.”

For Amon-Ra St. Brown, forging that strength has meant listening to Dad—and going old-school in the gym.

HOPEFULLY, HIS DAD DOESN’T READ THIS, but St. Brown hates to deadlift. He’d rather catch another 202 balls a day than do deadlifts. So he doesn’t really do them. “I just feel like deadlifts is a lot—a bunch of strain on your back,” he says.

“I’m not really flexible, so it’s hard for me sometimes to get that. Yeah, I don’t really do deadlifts.”

Never mind that the idea of skipping your deadlifts is something most fitfluencers could make hate-post #content about for daaaaaaays. St. Brown doesn’t care. “I just never really liked deadlifts,” he says, shrugging his shoulders.

It’s a reminder that building strength isn’t one-size-fits-all, especially at an NFL position that’s evolving, shaped by players who understand how to train for their own unique strengths. Hill, who’s known for his raw speed, regularly spends at least part of his offseason entering sprint competitions. Metcalf lifts Titan-level weights (and often does cleans, another move St. Brown hates), aiming to build pure power. And wideouts like A.J. Brown and Travis Hunter adopt hybrid strategies.

St. Brown’s strengths are versatility (did we mention he threw a TD last year?) and extreme resilience. He has missed just two games in his pro career and spent his second season playing through an oblique injury (something chronicled in Receiver). And both of those qualities were shaped by the no-nonsense parenting of former bodybuilder John Brown.

From the moment he and Miriam Brown had children, John had plans. He got the name for his oldest son, Equanimeous, from a friend’s book, then drew the names for his other sons from Egyptian lore (Osiris and Amon-Ra). He changed his sons’ last name to St. Brown, purely because the extra two letters would look good on a football jersey. And he insisted that they start training in the weight room by the time they were 5 years old. John pushed them too, driving Amon-Ra and his brothers through high-rep sets of bodybuilding exercises like bench presses and biceps curls—and making it all nonnegotiable. “You give them choice between chocolate, vanilla ice cream,” he says. “Not, do they play football or not? You make them do things that you know is good for them.”

Spoiler alert: He wanted them to play football. (And it worked: All three played receiver for big-name college squads, and Equanimeous and Amon-Ra remain in the NFL.) And Brown believed they needed to pack on muscle to survive those games. “Bodybuilding movements protect your exo-skeleton,” he says. “You take a lot of punishment throughout the season. You need muscles that are strong enough to stand that punishment.”

“You wanna get and make as many EXPLOSIVE PLAYS as you can.”

Throughout grade school and high school, Amon-Ra built classic muscle and strength with tried-and-true exercises like bench presses, squats, and biceps curls. And he added mental strength courtesy of his father’s penchant for advanced training techniques. Some days, Amon-Ra did 100-rep sets of exercises like leg extensions and leg curls. Others, he’d rep out biceps curls with a heavy weight, then lower the weight and do more reps. “I don’t believe in overtraining,” Brown says. “You can only under-sleep and under-eat.”

And Dad wasn’t about to let Amon-Ra skip leg day, the most grueling of all training days, either. The son had to squat, and the father made that extra intense. He’d often ask Amon-Ra to pick up a heavy weight, then he’d move the safety rack away from him. This ensured that the youngster had to squat deep, then power up with no margin for error. Amon-Ra got so comfortable squatting that he still does high-rep sets of it, even though most athletes prefer low-rep squat sets. “That’s a big thing of mine,” he says of the squat. “Keeping the foundation… that’s the back squat.”

Most athletes need some sort of deadlift-style lift in their workout, whether that’s a Romanian deadlift with dumbbells or a hip thrust. But St. Brown’s unconventional training plan has worked: The squats have thickened his quads and hamstrings, transforming him into a hybrid with running back strength and receiver speed. And that’s a key reason why he so easily shrugs off tacklers. That muscular lower body creates plenty of explosion, too: St. Brown has 60 catches of 20 yards or more since entering the NFL.

To fine-tune that quickness, he does explosive exercises. Since joining the Lions, St. Brown has done more speed-focused movements, like box jumps and power lunges.

“When I’m with the team, I do a lot more of those explosive movements,” he says. “I think there’s a time and a place to do both. I get a taste of both when I’m on my own. I lift the way I was raised, with kind of the bodybuilding style.”

And sure, it's unconventional and leads to ultra-intense offseason workout sessions. But it's worked, points out his father. "I was right again," Brown says. "So don't give your kids choices."

Unless they want to deadlift.

AMON-RA ST. BROWN CAN ADMIT IT. Dad made the right choices. After decades of catching 202 balls a day and battling through heavy reps of squats, Amon-Ra has strong hands and a rock-solid lower body, and he’s mentally tough enough for anything the game can throw at him.

And he’s playing the position that every team focuses on, and the one that plenty of the youngsters at his camp want to play. The best receiver from a family full of receivers spent a lifetime building strength to play the position. Now everyone wants in. St. Brown doesn’t mind. “A lot more people want to play receiver,” he says. “I’m happy to be in the thick of things.”

Amon-Ra-Geddon Leg Blaster

A typical athlete’s leg day isn’t exactly puke-worthy, often involving low-rep sets of exercises like squats and deadlifts. Not St. Brown. The Lion pushes his limits. Try his bodybuilding-inspired workout once a week for a month—and enjoy the leg #gains.

Set the machine to a light weight, then aim to do 70 (yes, really) reps. Focus on squeezing your quads tight on every rep and lowering until the weight stack hits the bottom. Immediately do your leg curls.

Set the machine to a light weight again, then aim to do 70 reps. Focus on flexing your hamstrings as much as possible, then lowering slowly. Rest 90 seconds, then go back to the leg extensions. Do 4 sets of this superset.

Now you’re doing Amon-Ra’s favorite exercise. Do 5 sets of 5 reps, resting 90 seconds between each set. Aim to work to a heavy weight. New to squats? Do goblet squats instead.

Do 3 sets of leg presses, aiming to do 10 reps during each set. On the third set, start with a heavy weight, do as many reps as you can, then lower the weight and immediately do 4 more reps. Lower the weight and immediately do 3 more reps.
