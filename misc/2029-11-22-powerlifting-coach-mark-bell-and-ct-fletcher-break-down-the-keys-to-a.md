# Powerlifting Coach Mark Bell and CT Fletcher Break Down the Keys to a Heavy Deadlift

The big three powerlifting exercises (deadlift, squat, and bench press) can be intimidating for the everyday lifter, especially once you advance beyond the basics and begin to build enough strength to work with big weights. The difference between a good lift and a great one is all in the technique—so it helps to really know what you're doing beyond the simple pull or push of the movement.

[CT Fletcher](https://regeneration-desk.hbsnzj.com/)

[Powerlifting Coach Mark Bell](https://memorycircuits.hbsnzj.com/)

Mark Bell, a legendary powerlifter, coach, and owner of the Super Training Gym, has shared his insights on the bench press before. Now, he's published a video clip from a deadlifting seminar featuring the illustrious powerlifter C.T. Fletcher, giving anyone who hopes to achieve a solid pull some actionable advice. As Bell and Fletcher share their knowledge with the audience, powerlifter and coach Nsima Inyang serves as a model, working up to a heavy pull.

How Mark Bell Teaches the Deadlift

The first important detail for a successful deadlift, according to Bell, is using the barbell to pull oneself into position. By doing this, you might see the bar "bending"—that shouldn't just be because you have a ton of plates stacked on either end. "You can do this with even a lot of the lighter weights," says Bell. "You can pull the slack out of the bar, get some tension on it."

To get into position, Bell advises to start with a hip hinge and bend to the bar, opening up your legs so that you can get a good grip without squatting. Push your hips back and reach instead, then pull on the bar to create tension, which will put you into what Bell calls a "mini squat."

Another point of focus is where your hips are in relation to your upper body. "The hips are going to be lower than your shoulders—that's going to be one of the key points of the deadlift." From there, keep your back flat and pull the slack out of the bar before you begin your rep.

Even though Bell has been lifting and coaching for years, he says he only learned one helpful cue for deadlifters within the last year. He calls it the "reverse shrug cue." Rather than engaging your shoulders to lift weights up, as you would in the shrug, flip that movement as you're gripping the barbell. "Just think of kind of pushing your arms downward the opposite way that you would shrug," he says. "You're going to feel the shoulder elongate, your chest and ribcage is going to pull up a little bit—that's exactly what we're trying to do when we're deadlifting."

[Powerlifting Coach Mark](https://980187.com/z2m246/)

Don't forget to engage your lats, either. "You're going to notice that it's very common amongst people that deadlift big weights is they have very large backs," Bell says, pointing to Inyang's especially broad back. "When you go to pull on that weight, those are supporting muscles that are involved in the deadlift."

Focusing on the Deadlift Setup

From there, they move to the demonstration. Inyang mentions that his biggest focus is on maintaining the same setup ritual for every single rep. "The less setup I have, the easier it is for me to do a good deadlift and keep it consistent from set to set and rep to rep," he says. "All the reps look the same from 135 to 650, 700, whatever I end up pulling." Bell concurs. "There's an old adage from Mike Tyson, he said that everyone has a plan until they get punched in the face," he says. "Well, everybody has a good way to lift some weights until the weights get to be really fucking heavy, and then all logic goes out the window... Develop a routine and try to stick with it."

Bell also cautions eager deadlifters to avoid lifting too much, too soon. Progression—and sticking to a program even if you want to go above and beyond its parameters—can be a recipe for disaster. "The weight have to be weights that your body is prepared for," he says. "Whatever the program is that you're about to do, your body has to be prepared for it in the best possible way—that will ensure that you get the greatest gains and the greatest results from that particular program. If you just jump into it out of nowhere, you most likely will get hurt." On that note, Bell encourages lifters to focus on training with submaximal weights (i.e. weights that aren't as heavy as your 1RM) so that you can hone your techniques within your means. Then, you can add the weights when you're ready.
