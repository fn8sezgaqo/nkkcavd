# Eric Dane on How He Prepared for Cal’s Intense 'Euphoria' Scene

Euphoria has a reputation for doing the most. It's depiction of teen angst is dialed up to 11, causing more than a few parents watching the show to panic just a little. But the messiest shenanigans of Season 2, Episode 4 didn't come from the show's young ensemble, but from one of their parents. Cal (played by Eric Dane) has long been the alpha dog of his community, but his machismo hides a lifelong struggle with his sexuality, which manifests as hook-ups with young gay men and trans women that he secretly films. His toxic masculinity has metastasized into his son Nate (Jacob Elordi), whose even more violent tendencies are rooted in his own struggles with sexual insecurity. This season, as Cal unravels on his warpath to retrieve a tape of his night with Jules (Hunter Schafer), the trans high school girl his son is secretly in love with, we're given a glimpse into his own youth in flashbacks. The summer before college, he and his best friend Derek begin to explore their feelings with each other, but are cut short when Cal's then-girlfriend Marsha announces that she's pregnant. Cal and Derek spend a night at a gay bar, and it's the only time we see Cal unfettered, unburdened, and free. When present-day Cal returns to the bar during a bender, we learn he hasn't stepped foot back into a space where he can openly be himself for nearly 25 years.

Dane read those flashbacks in the script, and then Euphoria creator Sam Levinson played him some footage from those scenes to help root adult Cal in his younger self. "I definitely wanted to match the physicality of the old Cal in that moment where he's remembering Derek so vividly [when he revisits the gay bar] that he actually sees him," Dane says. "At the same time, I'm kind of doing my own thing. Cal's a different guy than he was when he was 17 years old. So I was endeavoring to find the similarities between the different Cals in those moments."

Cal's arc hits a crescendo when he arrives home drunk in the middle of the night, promptly pees onto the hardwood floor, and proceeds to unload a lifetime of internalized trauma onto his wife and son. Much of it with his penis still out; the silent fifth member of the scene. The scene is long and devastating, as Cal vacillates between casual cruelty and delivering long-overdue truths. Dane bounces between tragedy and dark comedy, self-hatred and self-acceptance, and deep-cutting reads on not only his own secrets but those of his family. It's like watching an exorcism, but performed in the foyer of a McMansion.

"My first thought when I read the script was, I better start working on this right now! Because it's a lot of words," he says. "But I thought it made sense. I mean, you know, Cal is a deeply conflicted character. And he's been living this double-life for the better part of his adulthood. At some point, he's going to reach his breaking point." Men's Health talked to Dane about walking on a knife's edge for the scene, grappling with the expectations we have of masculinity in his own life, and the release of emotional unburdening yourself with you penis out.

Men's Health: Cal is the really the only adult character on the show who has a role comparable in size and depth to the younger ensemble. Why do you think he and his story get that distinction?

Eric Dane: The younger adults are fantastic and precocious and wonderfully talented actors. But I think the adults play an important role in this story too, because we have to see the dynamics and conditions the younger cast live under. And my son, Nate, is such a central figure in the greater story, and Cal was always going to be a central figure in this piece. I knew that from the beginning. He is such a deep and complex character, and I think it's interesting for Sam to explore. And I just think Sam likes me better than everybody.

When Cal unloads to his family, he talks a lot about being boxed in by the expectations of who he should be. At one point he tells his sons, “I’m not allowed to form emotional attachments. And I'm an emotional guy.” What do you think this character says about the ways we expect of men—particularly men currently in their 30s, 40s, and 50s—to behave?

I think we expect them to be completely together—stoic and centered and grounded, all these idealized traits that we want in the adults in our lives. And that's just not how things shake out. People are complicated, life is complicated. I thank God for this moment for Cal, because I think it needed to be said, and maybe now that everything is out in the open, he can have a truthful, honest relationship with his children.

Have you experienced these kinds of pressures yourself, both as a guy of a certain generation but also as an actor who often has to play a certain archetype of manhood?

I know what it's like to feel a certain way on the inside and look a different way on the outside. There have been times in my career where I've been put in roles because of what people perceive me to be. And, you know, it's just the exact opposite. I think everybody walks around with this inner battle that you know nothing about. In that sense, I know what it's like to live a double life—I get it. I understood this character from the jump.

[How He](https://thymicshield.dns0663.com/blog/2051614516.html)

One of the roles that you're most known for is Mark Sloan—or “McSteamy”—on Grey’s Anatomy. He was an idealized archetype of a man—the sex symbol with a heart of gold—seen through a largely female lens. Cal lives on the opposite end of that spectrum in many ways. What's it like to play these very different kinds of guys at different moments in your career?

It's been freeing for me to play this role. You know, I loved working on Grey's Anatomy, I loved playing Mark Sloan, and I never felt stilted—but I didn't feel like there was too much that was asked of me in that role. And Cal is a whole different story. There's a lot asked of me in this role, and the expectations are high. It’s incumbent on me to hit every note because Cal lives such a fragile kind of existence. This guy teeters on what is morally acceptable and what is legally acceptable. So it's important that I hit all the notes because otherwise he can so easily be misunderstood. There's a humanity to this character that I hope comes out. I don't expect people to love this character, but I hope people can have some empathy.

The tone of the scene between Cal and his family really toes the line between being utterly devastating and perversely funny.

Well, first of all, it's perfectly written. So a lot of it is just saying what's on the page and finding the truth in the circumstances. I was just talking about hitting different notes, and Sam is really good with the musicality of these scenes. He's a huge help in finding these beats that I wouldn't necessarily have identified. And my job is to sit there and stare at the other person, find a connection, and tell the truth.

The absurdity of the circumstances are right at the top of the scene—walking in and peeing on the floor of the foyer. Finding the absurdity and the hilarity in that moment was super liberating. And it really set a tone that allowed the character to kind of breathe and be honest.

I mean, look, Cal then tells Nate that his biggest regret is him. I don't think Cal wishes Nate wasn't born. I think what he’s saying is that he regrets the way he parented Nate. The adage “the truth hurts,” might apply here, but I think in some way, it could be the beginning of the healing process.

When you deliver that line, you and Jacob lock eyes and exchange a look. The camera cuts between the two of you, so we don’t see it all in one frame, but we feel it. How has your relationship with Jacob informed those kinds of moments?

Jacob and I are very close. Very close. And we have somewhat of a profound connection. When we look at each other, that connection is just available at all times. He's a fantastic actor who's so present and so engaged, it makes my job really easy. There’s another scene from last episode that also mixed humor and anxiety, when Cal goes to Fezco’s place—

And I get the shit beat out of me by Ashtray?

That’s the one! Cal tries so hard to be an alpha male. How did it feel to be beaten up repeatedly by an 11-year-old?

You know, it was fun! I took about 50 shots to the head with a prop shotgun. You know, Javon [Walton] is an athlete. The kid is a boxer, and he's going to be a professional athlete someday. He knows how to pull a punch and he was really great with that gun. So I walked away unscathed. But emotionally it is a little strange to see a child beat the crap out of you. Ashtray does not fuck around. That was really one of the one of the scenes that brought Cal to this breaking point. It was a wake-up call.

Despite all of the heavy material in the show, you've said before that the vibe on set is very much light and fun when the cameras aren't rolling. Do you guys ever break character on camera? I mean, you're delivering some devastating lines to Paula Marshall, who plays Marsha, with a prosthetic dick hanging out of your fly. How does she not crack?

I don't know, man. These guys are good. They stayed in character and they stayed connected for the for the duration of the day. We took the entire day to shoot that scene, which was somewhat exhausting, but also exhilarating.

Do you think Marsha has known or suspected the truth about Cal all these years?

No, I don't think Marsha knew. I think Cal has put up this ironclad façade, and nobody saw past it. He hadn't even been back to that gay bar in 20 years. He had shut that part of his life out with the exception of the secret..."rendezvous" he has in hotels with younger men and women.

I’ve got to ask about your prosthetic penis, which is hanging out of your fly for a large part of what is otherwise a very intense and devastating scene. How does one select a fake penis for a scene like this?

I had absolute control over the penis. Because it was supposed to be mine. I approved it. I said, “This looks like a nice penis. Let's use this one.”

Cal's a big swinging dick kind of guy. It was like a metaphor made literal. How much energy do they put into these into these, shall we say, visual metaphors?

More than you would think! But, you know, it's very freeing to do a monologue with your penis out. I hope everybody becomes a bit more comfortable with their appendages after this show.
