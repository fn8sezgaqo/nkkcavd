# Life Lessons from Dead People

The lab door is ajar, so I peek inside.

I have come to the medical sciences center at the University of Wisconsin at Madison to observe the dissection of human cadavers, and I want to see where they store the bodies. I'm trying to calibrate myself for the morning to come, but the odor in the hallway has me feeling a little hinky. The smell is faintly sweet, on the order of diluted molasses.

Over the years I've handled dead bodies at accident scenes, in the backs of ambulances, in funeral homes, and on autopsy tables, and I've never swooned or spewed -- but inhaling this hallway scent on an empty stomach is getting to me. It doesn't help that I missed breakfast and am staring at a wall of photos in which the human body is presented in cross sections resembling bacon. The pictures are titled "Slab One," "Slab Two," "Slab Three," and so on. There are a lot of slabs.

There isn't much else to see, really. The bodies -- eight of them -- are stored out of sight in horizontal steel tanks. The tanks are mounted on waist-high trolleys parked in two neat rows along either side of a room that's also decorated with dangling skeletons and several boxes of bones. A ventilation system sucks out formaldehyde fumes with a constant low-level whooosh.

One way or another, the dead people here are substituting for you and me. If you are healthy and hope to stay that way, at some point you will rely on the services of medical professionalswho learned their craft by cutting into a cadaver. Beginning with the Greek physician Herophilus in the third century B.C., cadavers have been used as teaching tools for doctors and dentists, allowing them to explore cavities, both oral and abdominal, without the fear that their learning curve could take a life.

More recently, however, demand for the deceased often outpaces supply -- occupational therapists, chiropractors, physical-education teachers, athletic trainers, anthropologists, and biomedical engineers often rely on corpses in the classroom. "That takes more cadavers out of the pool," says Richard Drake, Ph.D., secretary-treasurer of the American Association of Anatomists and the director of anatomy at the Cleveland Clinic Lerner School of Medicine at Case Western Reserve University. At the same time, there are regions of the country where fewer and fewer bodies are being donated -- resulting in the ironic situation that in some hospitals there aren't enough dead people. If tomorrow's doctors are going to save the living, they will require the services of the expired.

As we are about to hit the nitty-gritty, let us pause for a grace note. The dissection and study of a human corpse proceeds along a course of systematic physical destruction that, outside of a sanctioned educational context, is an affront to our most innate human sensibilities. That is to say, cutting up dead people is generally a no-no. And yet it is impossible, even when taking apart a fellow human being, to remain perpetually reverential. I am happy, then, that before I ever laid eyes on a cadaver-cutting session, I made my way to Philadelphia's Irvine Auditorium for the Humanity Gifts Registry (HGR) Celebration of Remembrance.

I attended at the invitation of Bruce Hirsch, Ph.D., an associate professor of anatomy at the Drexel University college of medicine and the president of HGR, a nonprofit organization that coordinates the receipt and distribution of bodies to medical and dental schools in Pennsylvania. Dressed in a black suit, white shirt, and black bow tie, Hirsch spoke from the stage, addressing the several hundred friends and relatives of individuals who had died and become donors that year.

"The basis of medicine is still to be found in an understanding of how we are made," he said. "Those we remember today have left for their legacy the health and well-being of those who remain." He then turned the program over to the students seated behind him. They wore white lab coats and sat in neat rows. Over the next hour they delivered eulogies, played classical music, sang, offered prayers, and read poems for the people they had come to know -- bone by bone, fiber by fiber -- over the previous months.

"I've been teaching anatomy for 30 years now, and you become used to it, but you are still surrounded by dead people," said Hirsch later. "The ceremony reminds me that this is not routine. That for the students as well as the families, there are real emotional issues."

Finally, by turns, the students made their way to the podium to read the names of those the families had come to honor. There were 280 names in all. The students read them firmly, and with reverence. There were sniffles and there were smiles, but mostly the families listened with quiet pride. Out here in the hallway with the funny smell, I am carrying that with me: The idea that each body in the dissection room was given through the conscious decision of a charitable person. That no matter the frank nature of what is about to transpire, what you have here ultimately is the opening of a gift.

Nothing dissipates a deathly atmosphere as quickly as the squeak and slap of flip-flops. I hear the students before I see them, and then they come piling into the lab, slinging their backpacks on the floor against the wall. They don white lab coats and fit fresh blades into their scalpels, chattering all the while. Edward Bersu, Ph.D., a professor of anatomy and the course instructor, has placed a bowl of Tootsie Rolls just inside the door, and several students dip in.

Now comes a hinge squawk and then a bang as the tank covers are opened, followed by a faint chain-rattle as the cadavers are reeled up from the formaldehyde depths. Each body is wrapped in a shroud, which the students peel away, leaving the head and face covered.

"Hey!" a female student says. "They took our side!" (Each cadaver is shared by two lab sections: One group is given the left side, the other the right side.) Someone points out that the student is standing at the wrong end of the table -- she's at the foot, rather than at the head. "Oops, never mind," she giggles.

The assignment today is simple, but bracing: Skin one leg, and expose the nerves and muscles of the posterior abdominal wall. There are four or five students at each cadaver, and they take turns with the scalpel and forceps, slicing the skin into strips and peeling it away, being careful not to nick the underlying fascia. Separated from the body, the skin is a bloodless, yellow-tan, and not at all lifelike, but it's immediately recognizable as the real deal, complete with fine hair and wrinkles.

Many of the students are wearing ball caps stitched with the red University of Wisconsin "W" logo. One capless guy has sunglasses propped on his shaved head, and there is a lot of gum chewing. The conversation is equally informal.

"Hey, what bone is this?"

"I just don't like drinking at 8 a.m. I used to, but . . . "

"Oops!"

"Hey, here's the greater sarsaparilla vein!"

The last student is actually pointing his forceps at the greater saphenous vein, and if it seems unseemly to play word games over dead bodies, then it's worth noting that a 2005 study from King's College in London shows that telling jokes around the dissection table helps medical students get to know each other, relax, and become comfortable with the idea of the human body as something to be tinkered with. The study also reveals that by facing up to a dead body, students learn how to maintain the "professional distance" required to make decisions based on evidence rather than emotion.

"Even before entering an anatomy laboratory, the student, at some level, knows that the first person he will care for is a dead one," wrote Mohammed Aziz, Ph.D., an associate professor of anatomy at the Howard University college of medicine, in a 2002 paper on the importance of dissection in medical training.

In addition to basic anatomy, cadavers are used to train medical students to perform lifesaving invasive procedures for which computer models, plastic mannequins, and animal stand-ins don't provide an adequate substitute. Cadavers also move medicine forward. "When there's a new surgical procedure, it's tested on donated bodies," says L. Ebony Boulware, M.D., a Johns Hopkins University researcher who has studied the public's attitudes toward organ, tissue, and body donation. A myriad of medical milestones, from heart transplants to hip replacements, would have been delayed by damaging false starts -- or never reached at all -- had it not been for the rehearsal space provided by genuine humans.

For example, before completing the first partial face transplant in U.S. history, the Cleveland Clinic's surgical team conferred with researchers at Duke University who were preparing for the first full transplant by removing the faces of four cadavers, and then playing mix-and-match to assess "morphological compatibility." In other words, would it be possible to drape Jeremy Piven's face over Hugh Jackman's cheekbones and achieve a satisfactory result?

In the course of his 35-year career, Bersu has sorted through thousands of bodies, but he still conveys wonder. "Oh! Oh! This is rare!" he says, bent almost double, both hands inside a cadaver. "The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve! Can you see it?" As the students lean in, he tenderly teases away the fragile nerve -- usually destroyed during blunt dissection -- from the underlying musculature. With his other hand, he scoops out gobs of fat. The students wear gloves, but Bersu is bare-handed. Instructing the students to expose the nerve further, he backs away, wipes his hand on his lab coat, grabs his coffee cup from the scalpel tray, and scoots off.

At the foot of Tank Two, a dissection guide is splayed over a stool, open to a page captioned "Nerves of Posterior Abdominal Wall." I look from the book to the pasty insides of the cadaver. "You just can't compare a textbook rendering to the real thing," says one of the students. She's using a turkey baster to remove excess formalde-hyde from the abdominal cavity. "Cadavers also let you see the anomalies, like how much the same structure can vary from person to person. Unlike with the books, where every pancreas looks the same."

Even experienced doctors benefit from seeing the natural variation of real bodies. During optic-nerve decompression (surgery to restore the vision of head-injury victims), surgeons rely on common anatomical landmarks to avoid damaging the extremely delicate nerves required for sight. But in a 2008 study at the Shanghai Jiao Tong University school of medicine, researchers performed the procedure on eight cadaver heads and noted the absence of one critical landmark in 38 percent of the eyes.

"What we see here makes it so much easier to connect when you're working with a real patient," says one student. Five minutes ago, she'd been trying to distinguish a cadaver's foot flexors from its extensors by leaning against the tank and wiggling her own foot. "You can visualize the interior structures you'll be treating. When you see how so many tendons are crammed through the smallest little space, and how much muscle pull they support, then you understand something like tennis elbow or carpal tunnel syndrome."

Sam Krier, a teaching assistant, agrees that what he has seen in the lab helps prepare him for the real world. "If you're going to work with someone who has had a total knee replacement, there's nothing like seeing a total knee replacement," he explains. "We also need to see what an osteoporotic bone looks like. Or an arthritic joint. Move it, and feel it grind."

Not all students have the privilege. And as one female student piped up to say, the substitutes are no substitute: "My first college was small, so we used cats!"

Bersu and his students aren't in the business of diagnosis, but they indulge in a little medical speculation. "We think this guy died of a heart attack," says a young male student. "He had a lot of rib fractures, like someone had done CPR on him." "But they could have been old fractures," says another student. "No," says the first student, "there's fresh blood around them."

Meanwhile, at the adjacent tank, Bersu is examining a large male cadaver. The students know the man was a farmer; his upper arms and shoulders are thickly muscled, but his legs are contrastingly skinny. "I suspect he was in a wheelchair for some time," says Bersu, pointing out the atrophied sartorius and calf muscles. "That's why you see this muscle wasting in the lower limbs. . . . Oh!" This last exclamation comes as he spots some unusual structures on the man's shins. Dark and hard, they look like bits of petrified angleworm. "Subcutaneous cysts," he says, dislodging one with his fingernail.

"What is this?" asks a female student from a table in the far corner. Bersu trots over, and the student drops something in his palm. It looks like a wet soybean. She'd dug it from the cadaver's buttock. "Hmmm," says Bersu. The student has sliced open one end of the object, and Bersu studies the cross section carefully. "It's calcified," he says. They speculate that it might be a lymph node, but ultimately he declares it to be just another cyst.

Some cadavers bear the obvious marks of disease and hard living: spotted livers, healed bones, old scars. An ankle broken decades ago still screwed together by a steel plate, the hardware incongruously shiny against the yellow-tan bone. A heart the color of overboiled corned beef and as big as a cabbage (a classic sign of an overworked, failing heart) and criss-crossed with heart-bypass vein grafts that look like pale noodles stitched in place.

A female student draws my attention to a blackened lung that has been removed from a cadaver and placed beside its knee. Cadaver organs are paler than those you see during surgery or even during the autopsy of a recently deceased person, but this lung is still obviously dark and seamed with tar. "That's all you need to know about smoking," she says.

An hour and a half into the session, Bersu shows no signs of slowing. Sipping from his coffee, he shuttles from tank to tank. "Oh, that's nice!" he says, addressing a group of students who have painstakingly exposed their cadaver's saphenous vein. It hangs draped along the inside of the thigh like a sodden clothesline. Bersu points out how smaller communicating veins exit the main vein on the perpendicular at regular intervals and then disappear into the deep muscle tissue.

"When you see this, you can understand how long periods of inactivity lead to blood pooling," he says. "If you stand still for a long time, the blood just sits in this large diameter vein, but if you move, your muscles squeeze and relax around these smaller veins and create pressure changes that move the blood around." Bersu illustrates his point with an anecdote from his military days. After watching numerous buddies pass out while standing at attention on a blistering tarmac waiting for the arrival of President Nixon, he says he maintained consciousness and military decorum by training himself to flex his legs without breaking the crease of his dress slacks.

Earlier, I'd heard Bersu describe the sound a sclerotic artery makes when it is crushed. I ask if he can give me an example. "Oh, sure! Let's see, I think Tank Three over there, no . . . let's see, oh yes, Tank Two!"

The cadaver is flat on its back, and the abdomen has been scooped clean, giving full access to the spot where the descending aorta splits to go down each leg. Bersu points to the bifurcation. "The split creates turbulence eddies, and that's where plaque builds up, just like silt in a river." He steps aside and ushers me in. "Feel that common iliac artery -- it's nice and crunchy!" I put out one gloved index finger and press against the artery, and indeed, I hear a faint, soggy crackle. I am thinking it feels like crushing eggshells under a wet dishrag when Bersu says, "Cardiovascular surgeons call that Eggshell Alley. Now think about what happens if a piece of that plaque shears off -- pretty soon your leg turns cold, then painful. . . ."

As he goes on to describe the grim consequences of a deep-vein embolism, it's as if he's revealing these things to an audience for the first time -- never mind the thousands of common iliac arteries he has seen. Bersu credits his students for sustaining his demeanor over three decades of instruction. "It's fun, especially when the great majority of students are very enthusiastic."

They have to be. Human dissection is no place for the reticent. One of today's assignments requires students to expose a nerve hidden deep in a muscle. After watching one student peck away, Bersu moves in. "You want to take the forceps and shred the muscle." He demonstrates, and pulls the muscle out in strips, depositing them in a little pile beside the body. Reflexively, I think pulled pork.

At another table, two students are leaning over their work when one of them jumps back. "Oh! I just got splattered!"

"Holy crap-a-moley!"

"Ewww!"

A tuft of gray hair protrudes from beneath the shroud covering the face of the cadaver beside me. I look at the hands then, imagine them working a comb. At the ceremony in Philadelphia, one student recalled a cadaver with pink fingernail polish. Every time he pulled back the shroud and prepared to cut, the pink polish reminded him of the woman who had lived in the body, and how her final act was one of generosity.

The time passes quickly, and soon it is noon. Just as fast as they appeared, the bodies are back in the tanks, and the students are off to lunch. The room is silent. And all the Tootsie Rolls are gone.

A dissected human body is fascinating. But it is not reassuring. Jumbled in the empty cavity, organs that once squeezed and pulsed and glistened suddenly take on the appearance of pieces of a discarded 3-D puzzle, which in fact they are. The undone corpse sends an overarching message: You are meat and bones and bits of skack. In the mirror you may be sleek and tweaked, but inside you are a squishy contraption strung together over a skeleton.

And yet as I leave the lab, the thing that sticks with me isn't the chill of mortality, but rather the echoes of all the chatter and movement that animated this room for 2 hours. Peeking back in at the tanks, I think of the millions moldering in their graves while these cadavers are surrounded by so much life.
