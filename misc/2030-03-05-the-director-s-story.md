# The Director's Story

When it all started, I was the happiest 32-year-old kid in the world. I felt childlike elation as I sank my teeth into that first bite of McBliss.

My anticipation had spiked when I'd set foot in the door of the McDonald's on 14th Street in Manhattan and hit that wall of unmistakable aroma that can only mean McDonald's: a combination of deep-fried sizzle and cleaning solvents, of burgers and plastic. It smelled good. But this olfactory extravaganza would haunt me like a curse for the next 29 days.

I'm a filmmaker and television producer. I'm also a regular guy. I love burgers. I love steaks. I love pig flesh in its multitudinous prepared forms. Though I seldom ate at McDonald's, I certainly had nothing against the place. When I was growing up, I rarely ate the stuff. My mother said fast food wasn't real food. But as an adult, if I was traveling and there was nowhere else to eat, I'd head for the golden arches like any other American. (There are more than 13,000 McDonald's restaurants in the United States.) Same thing if I was hungover; there's nothing like the Number 2 Value Meal to make my drunken stomach happy --perhaps the additional abuse takes the edge off.

There was a little pooch at my midsection, but otherwise I was in pretty good shape: 6'2'', 185.5 pounds, precisely. I had written down my weight in a loose-leaf binder because I suspected things might change.

I was embarking on a feature-length documentary, Super Size Me, in which I would eat nothing but McDonald's food for 30 days straight. Why? I wanted to illustrate dramatically what is happening in America. The fast-food culture has utterly changed how we eat and, more important, how much we eat. It has helped make us fatter--especially children--and sicker. I wanted to see (and show) what it would do to my body. And I knew it would make a good movie.

So here it was, 0900 hours, McD-Day. I went straight to the counter and ordered the breakfast classic familiar to every American: the Egg McMuffin. A little juice dribbled down my chin as I chewed. I was happy.

On the second day, I puked my guts out. Okay, it was my fault--the result of a fat overload as I tried to eat my first Super Size Meal. My body wasn't ready for the 42 ounces of Coke, the nearly half-pound sleeve of fries, and the Double Quarter Pounder with Cheese.

But I recovered. The show would go on.

The idea for my adventure came to me in 2002, when you couldn't open a magazine or turn on the television without hearing about America's obesity epidemic. On Thanksgiving of that year, I had assumed my traditional postdinner position--slumped on my mother's couch--when a spokesman for McDonald's came on the tube, defending its menu. Through my turkey-induced haze, a lightbulb clicked on.

I'll admit, I have a fondness for the extreme. The MTV show I Bet You Will was mine. We'd go around with a camera and challenge people to do crazy things, like sell us the clothes off their backs. This would be different--I'd take the challenge: Eat at McDonald's every day for a month. Outrageous, but no more so than a McSpokesman claiming their food is nutritious.

We'd film the meals, record my weight, check with doctors periodically, and see what happened.

And we did. The movie premiered at the Sundance Film Festival in January. I was awarded the Best Director prize in the documentary competition, and we sold the movie to a big-time distributor.

Two weeks after Sundance, McDonald's denounced the film as a "supersized distortion," saying it wasn't about its food, it was about what happens when you ingest 5,000 calories a day. About a month later, it announced that by the end of the year there would be no more supersizing in its U.S. shops. Coincidence?

I think not. The official reason: menu simplification. (We all know how confusing that menu can be, don't we?) McDonald's also said that the decision had nothing whatsoever to do with the film. Fine, whatever.

For my girlfriend, Super Size Me was a horror movie. Alexandra is a vegan and a chef. When I told her my idea for the project, she was aghast. When I began it, she was repelled. When I finished, she was scared.

I traveled around the country making the movie, from California to Texas to Wisconsin, hitting drive-thrus in rented minivans.

By Day 10, I had eaten everything on the menu, and my body was in rebellion. I had been through the hamburger, the cheeseburger, the Big Mac and the Big N' Tasty, the Quarter Pounder and the Double Quarter Pounder with Cheese, the Filet-O-Fish, the McChicken, the Crispy Chicken, the Chicken McNuggets, the Chicken McGrill, the McRibs and the McGriddles (two products from the McLab that I admit I still McLike), every flavor of soda and shake, plus salads, apple pies, and strawberry sundaes. And yes, I had fries with all of that.

My doctors got worried pretty quickly. I gained 18 pounds in 12 days, and my cholesterol and blood pressure climbed. Scariest of all: One measure of the fat content of my liver increased by 2,000 percent. "Your liver is now like pate," said one doctor. He feared I was headed for NASH, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, better known as "fatty liver," a condition that can lead to cirrhosis and liver failure. It can occur as a result of extreme weight gain or poor diet, among other things.

I was getting headaches that were alleviated only when I ate more. I felt depressed and exhausted most of the time. I was depressed, that is, except when I was eating. You heard correctly--the minute I started eating, my mood lifted, and I would remain elated for about 45 minutes. Then I would crash again, as depressed as before, only now with an anchor of sugar-covered fat in my stomach.

My knees and ankles ached from the extra weight, and I wheezed when I walked up stairs. I constantly wanted to nap. I felt unfocused, getting dumber by the day. I forgot things that were just told to me, and I couldn't remember things I'd known for years. My skin at first became splotchy and red, then ashen. My sexual desire drooped--I couldn't get a full erection to save my life. Luckily, I had a very patient girlfriend. If I'd been in search of a mate at the time, I would have had no chance.

Of course, the weight gain was all my fault--it was self-inflicted gluttony and sloth. (And the same thing probably would have happened to me if I'd eaten at Burger King or Wendy's.) I walked about 2 miles a day but never exercised beyond that, to mimic the behavior of most Americans. But, for millions of Americans, I think my example can serve as a warning, or at least a counterweight to fast-food advertising.

Fast-food chains say their offerings are "sometime" foods and that they're "part of a balanced diet." I find this laughable. Your diet is balanced not because you eat their food, but because of the nutritious foods you have outside the restaurant: fresh fruits and vegetables, whole grains, legumes, lean meats, and low-fat dairy products. You never hear one of these "restaurants" tell you how often you should eat their food. The last thing they want to do is suggest that you shouldn't go there as often as you want.

Yes, what I did was extreme. Yes, I sometimes ate nearly 5,000 calories a day, about twice what my body needed. But I feel my experiment was no more extreme than spending billions on advertising to get parents and children to eat fattening food. It's not really the burgers and fries I'm worried about; it's the amounts they push, and the amounts we eat.

I don't advise anyone to attempt what I did. I know that many of you eat fast food on a regular basis. I'm not saying what happened to me will happen to you. But look at my symptoms and see if you recognize any small-scale versions of them.

Many of my friends talk about how tired they feel. Now I see the parallels with what I went through. They aren't eating burgers and fries three times a day, but they are eating heavy, fat-filled foods, then washing them down with sugary sodas and iced teas. Even sports drinks are loaded with sugar to give them the "kick" you're looking for.

At the end of my experiment, I weighed 210 pounds. The first step toward getting my body back was to stop drinking my calories. On my McDiet, I was imbibing more than 1,000 calories a day just in sodas and shakes. Want to kick-start your diet? Go all-water for 2 weeks; you'll be amazed. Alexandra put me on a diet of nutrient-dense foods that kept me satisfied and helped restore my sanity. Slowly, the weight dropped off.

It was more than a year after I finished my experiment that I returned to my normal weight. Today, I still eat burgers (although not McDonald's), but probably just once a month. It's weird--ever since I did this, my body seems to put on weight more readily than it ever has. Is it possible that I've grown more fat cells, making me prone to regain weight easily? Maybe--so I pay close attention to what goes in my mouth. I'm in the gym three or four times a week, and I ride my bike to work almost every day.

Would I ever eat McDonald's again? In the film, we surveyed 100 nutritionists, and most of them said you could eat fast food once a month. By that standard, I've eaten enough fast food to last me 8 years. So, yeah, I probably will eat it again someday--I'll meet you there in May 2012.
