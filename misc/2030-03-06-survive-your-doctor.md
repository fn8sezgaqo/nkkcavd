It had been a busy day, with the usual range of sore backs and sore throats and people whose dogs had somehow managed (again!) to eat all their prescription narcotics. By the time I met the man with the cold, he'd been waiting in an exam room for 2 hours. The charge nurse had put him there when he'd started coughing in the waiting room. The triage nurse wasn't at fault. He just didn't look that sick. Did he minimize his complaints? Was his failure to mention his HIV status a part of that minimizing, or was he afraid of what would happen if he told? Was it just that, miserable and sick, he sat quietly, conserving his strength for each breath? He just didn't look that sick.

I'll never know. But I've made a mental note to myself: Next time I come to the hospital with pneumonia, I'm going to cough a lot and make a fuss. Or maybe not. Because there are so many ways to present yourself to your doctor. And so many of them, in that brief moment when you lay eyes on each other for the first time, can go wrong.

The Doorway Diagnosis

Whenever I enter a room with a patient in it, I try to stop in the doorway, and before anything else happens, I take in the scene. I want a look before my impression can be biased by information or personality or anything but the overall clinical picture. Most doctors do this, or try to. It's called the doorway survey, and it can be the most important moment of your appointment. That moment in the doorway provides the context for everything else that happens, steering your doctor as he or she tries to figure out which parts of your story to pay attention to, which parts to ignore, which parts need explanation, and which parts speak for themselves.

But, as important as they are, first impressions can be wrong. Under the ferocious pressure of the clock that drives most doctors' days, the time from doorway to diagnosis can be a handful of minutes. Under these conditions, the temptation to lump patients into categories is huge. We group people by what we already know about them, so the patient sitting on the examining table is nothing more or less than his ruined lungs, his cirrhotic liver, his diabetes. Which is fine, until the diabetic surprises you with something new and unrelated: a nasty vasculitis, or a cancer. With the diagnostic process so foreshortened, that initial impression looms large. If it's wrong, you can end up being categorized in ways that can do you harm.

"Are you thirsty? Urinating more often?"

His eyes get wide. "All the time. I'm drinking, what, 20 cups of coffee a day!"

And so it goes, on through the entire list, most of which he's sure he's had. By the time I get to "Any swollen glands, sores that don't heal, unusual bruises?" (vigorous nods), I haven't learned anything except that this guy is worried about his health. And that both of us could use a vacation.

If there's anything that sets the medical profession's collective teeth on edge, it's the patient with the pan- positive review of systems. There are some good reasons why we hate this sort of thing. Clinically, it can be hard to tell which of the yeses are significant. We worry that, in the general uproar of mysterious tinglings and funny moles and deathly fatigue, we're missing something important.

The other reason we dislike this kind of presentation isn't quite so disinterested. Despite everything, most doctors are motivated by a genuine concern for people. When you tell me you're suffering, on some level I'm moved, and even though I've been trained to keep that emotion carefully locked up, it's there. When I find somebody exploiting my sympathies with a display of bogus distress, I feel a little used. It shouldn't make a difference, really; I'm also trained to ignore my own petty grievances. It shouldn't make a difference. But I'll bet it does.

A Deeper Problem

In the end, I settle the matter by the direct test. I leave him alone for a few minutes (we both need a break) and rummage through the lunchroom for a paper bag. When I bring the bag into the examination room, my patient looks at it with a mixture of suspicion and dread.

"Here. Put this over your nose and mouth and breathe."

In a few minutes, I ask him, "How are you feeling?"

He looks at me over the bulging wad of brown paper.

"It's gone!" He holds his hands out in front of him and turns them over, marveling.

As diagnostic tests go, it could have been worse. MRIs, spinal taps—you name it, he could have gotten it. All the paper bag did was trap his CO2; as he rebreathed it, his acid/base balance recovered, and the tingling went away. Diagnosis and cure in the same brown bag. But the point isn't what a swell diagnostician I am. It's what didn't happen in the encounter.

This guy, I'd told myself in the doorway, wasn't for real. Everything he'd done in our conversation had confirmed that doorway diagnosis: His complaint was, if not imaginary, trivial. His review of systems was just impossible. And now, having cured him, I was happy to let him and his overwrought imagination go back to his hellish job. Which is where my doorway survey let him down.

What I neglected to do was ask my patient what had really brought him in, the real medical problem of which the tingling was a symptom. I made the classic mistake: I treated a symptom and told myself I'd cured the disease. For starters, this man needed to cut back on his coffee consumption. Then he needed to quit that job. And if he couldn't do that, I needed to help him find ways of dealing with his job stress—the first step of which might have been to give it a name, a start toward understanding what his situation was doing to him.

But I didn't do any of that. My initial assessment, confirmed by his active imagination, had blinded me to the glaringly obvious fact that the pan-positive review was itself a symptom.

Symptoms of Lies

He's here for a routine physical. Thirty-seven years old, strong family history of coronary-artery disease, high blood pressure, diabetes. His chief complaint today, the nurse's note says, is "fatigue." From the doorway, I see a man who's pale, slightly slumped, a little sad maybe, but clearly in no distress. He's about 70 pounds overweight. Obese, I tell myself. I move heavily into the room, anticipating another fruitless discussion of a diet that goes nowhere.

"Tell me about the fatigue."

There's nothing unusual about the story. At the end of the day, after a job that involves nothing more strenuous than a trip to the copy machine, he comes home from work dead tired, plunks himself on the couch, and stays there until it's time to go to bed.

"Is there something wrong with me, Doc?"

It's perched there on the tip of my tongue, but I rein it in. Better if he comes to it himself.

"Tell me about your diet. Do you watch what you eat?"

"Oh, yeah. I'm trying to lose weight."

"Tell me what you've been eating."

He tells me. A very strict diet. Bowl of oatmeal for breakfast. Microwavable meal at lunch. Another at dinner. By his accounting, he's getting about 900 calories a day. At a rough approximation, even at his low level of activity, he ought to be losing more than a pound a week. In fact, he's gained 15 since I saw him last year.

Something doesn't match up here: Either my view from the doorway is skewed, or he's leaving something out. He can lie to himself, I figure. But why does he have to lie to me?

He's lying to me, of course, because I'm the one asking all these embarrassing questions. Patients lie to me all the time. Perhaps the most consistent lie is how much they eat, followed by how much they drink, how much they smoke, and how much they exercise. (Number of sexual partners is another notoriously fabulous area.) If the picture I get from the doorway is of a man 70 pounds overweight, what you tell me about what you eat and how far you walk shouldn't matter. But it does. For all our cynicism (and I wonder, really, if we're as cynical as we claim to be), doctors live in the same web of language and persuasion, manners and emotion as everyone else. If you tell me you're eating only 300 calories at every meal, no matter how unlikely it is, that's still going to cloud my thinking, maybe keep me from saying what needs to be said—which is, "You've got a problem with food. Let's deal with it."

But is that really what needs to be said? A lie to the doctor can be a kind of a plea: Don't humiliate me, don't expose me, don't make me face what I'm trying to hide. But there's one thing about this patient that isn't a lie: He's here. He came in for a reason.

I've missed again. What I thought I saw from the doorway wasn't the whole picture. I chose to look at the flesh but missed the man. Between that fantasy report of perfect self-control and the undeniable reality of a man whose control falls short of his desires, there's something important. A lie, yes, but also a sadness that, if I had kept my mind open a minute or two longer, the two of us might have explored. A lie is not only a plea; it's a symptom, too.

Role Reversal

The most dreadful view from the doorway, however, is us.

From the door, the first thing I can see is that the doctor on the table looks terrible. There's a bad color in his face. He's lost weight. He looks worried.

"Hi," I say cautiously. "How are you?"

"Not good," he says, and I almost recoil. He's one of the older clinicians in another department. I've known him since I was in medical school. When he asked me to be his doctor, I was flattered. I don't think I've seen him in my clinic more than once since then. But he comes in for a last-minute appointment, with a story we both know the meaning of before it's half told: weight loss, nausea, a gnawing pain below his breastbone. And he's been overtaken by a sense of impending doom. I look at him and feel a powerful urge to back away. This room has become a very dangerous place.

"I've been trying to ignore it," he says. "But this morning, as I was going in to see my first patient, I burst into tears."

I almost cry out, "You did what?" I catch it just in time, manage to turn it into a noncommittal "Hmmm?"

"I just had to excuse myself and get out. It took me 5 minutes before I could go back in." He waits, but I don't say anything. "I told the poor guy I'd just heard some bad news. He spent most of the slot trying to reassure me." He stops and stares toward the eye chart on the wall, blinking hard. "I hate it when they try to take care of you."

He collects himself again. We agree on what we need to do, and I order the CT scan that will show us the mass we both know is growing in his pancreas. As I leave the room, I stop and turn back. "I'll call you with the results," I say. "Damn right," he shoots back.

I stand in the doorway another second or two, watching him look at me. That's me in 20 years, I tell myself. He looks back at me across that gulf of years, and for a brief moment, I think I know what he's feeling.

More Than a Diagnoses

The doorway survey is an important moment, because in that brief pause, in the gaze the doctor fixes on the human being on the table, everything is up for grabs. The nature of the relationship between those two people begins to solidify then and there. If the gaze is simply the clinical stare, one set of rules starts to play out. There may be skill and knowledge, a certain perception brought to bear. You, the patient, may get what you came for. But if in that moment I can see you on that table as something other than a diagnosis waiting to be made, as someone connected to me by something more mutual than mere doctoring, we have a chance, each of us, to work together on a kind of healing that makes us human to each other once again.

I'm not suggesting that this is your responsibility. When you come to see me, just bring yourself—what you know, what you feel, whatever it is you are that day. That's your job.
