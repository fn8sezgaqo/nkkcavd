BRIE LARSON WON our hearts before a single Golden Globe nomination was announced. In Lessons In Chemistry, one of Apple TV+'s best shows, Larson plays meticulous chemist-turned-trailblazing 1960s cooking show host Elizabeth Zott as she handles grief, motherhood, sexism, and scientifically-perfected lasagna. The streamer's adaptation of Bonnie Garmus's novel of the same name has eight episodes that will snatch the breath out of your lungs and drain the tears from your eyes. By the third episode, Larson delivered an acting performance that deserves to be celebrated in awards gold. And she didn't need to speak a single word to do so.

Before we set the scene, let's explore the stakes. Over the first two episodes, Elizabeth is an underappreciated lab tech at Hastings Research Institute whose genius is victimized by patriarchy. She has to pencil in "Miss" in the application for the Remsen Foundation Grant, which rewards chemists for outstanding achievements, because only a "Mr." is expected to be brilliant. Years earlier, she wasn't allowed to finish her Ph.D. studies at UCLA because she refused to apologize for freeing herself by stabbing her mentor Dr. Bates (Tate Ellington) while he was raping her. Her scientific credentials are invalidated because of her gender, and her work is copied because of its brilliance.

One of her only supporters is Calvin Evans (Lewis Pullman), a famous chemist at Hastings who sees Elizabeth as her whole self. He was more intrigued than furious when he learned she was stealing his precious ribose as an unknown lab tech to him to do research after hours. He was in awe of (and highly turned on by) her mind, and how it made a breakthrough connection in theories he wasn't ready to share with the world because they were incomplete. He abandoned his vision of their future as a married couple with a kid for hers when she told him, "A woman has to make a choice: Does she want to have children, or does she want to do anything else?"

Unfortunately, both versions of their future were cut short by his tragic passing at the end of the second episode. Not long after Calvin's death, Zott vomited one night and checked her notes to find out she hadn't had her period yet. So, she did what any scientist would: ran an experiment to determine if she was pregnant by injecting a frog with her urine. What happened next was a transformation that should secure Larson a few golden statues.

The Best Lessons In Chemistry Scene

As her frog experiment sits on her kitchen table, Elizabeth prepares dinner in a pensive trance where you can almost see her supercomputer brain simulate all of the possible futures that could come from her laboratory pregnancy test results. Her seasonings are in test tubes, and what appears to be cooking oil is in a beaker. Every minute facet of her life is inextricable to her ambition.

As soon as she took dinner out of the oven, her eyes nervously slid from the food to over her shoulder where the two frogs—her control frog with none of her urine and experimental frog with her urine—sat. But, the duet of ribbits has been reduced to a solo, and the pensiveness in her eyes turns to concern. As she delicately lifts the sheet covering the two glass containers with the frogs, she looks intently at the experimental frog and how it lay immobilized with eggs descending in the water. Elizabeth realizes her urine prompted ovulation in the frog, confirming she is pregnant. The shock overwhelms her, but the small details Larson demonstrates at this moment convey so much by saying nothing.

She twitches her eyelids as if her brain is malfunctioning, reminding us Elizabeth finds comfort in predictability, whether it's scientifically tested recipes producing the same lasagna or a chemical reaction. Larson's face droops sullenly, mouth agape, hope drained. At this moment, the audience knows she's had to work twice as hard to get half the respect as a scientist, and quietly concedes to each patriarchal ceiling placed on her potential—all while visibly bubbling with anger inside. That anger returns seconds after this startling revelation, but Elizabeth doesn't accept the future her pregnancy seemingly predetermines.

First, she grabs her glasses and a sledgehammer and unleashes her frustration by destroying the same kitchen countertop she had just prepared dinner on. There's a pain in each of her swings but also defiance. She won't allow herself to only be a domesticated mother. She won't allow herself only to do what society expects of her. As we find out moments later, when neighbor Harriet Sloane (Aja Naomi King) interrupts her cathartic demolition with a knock on the door, Elizabeth is "building an industrial-grade chemical lab" because if the world doesn't give her the life she deserves, she'll build it herself.

In one scene, Larson had a death and a rebirth. She conveyed the loss of a future Elizabeth always wanted with a catatonic gaze and a resurgence that almost pulsated her chest wide open. Pain, shock, hope, defiance, and determination coursed through every facial expression, exasperated yell, and sledgehammer smash. Larson displayed her award-winning emotional control by not relying on expertly crafted dialogue to tell the audience what was on Elizabeth's mind; she showed them. And, in turn, showed everyone who votes for the Golden Globes that the Best Actress in a Miniseries or Television Film award should be hers and hers alone.
