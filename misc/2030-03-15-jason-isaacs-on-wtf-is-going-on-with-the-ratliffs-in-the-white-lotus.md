# Jason Isaacs on WTF Is Going on With the Ratliffs in 'The White Lotus'

The following story contains spoilers for The White Lotus season 3, episode 6, "Denials."

JASON ISAACS HAS played all kinds throughout his nearly four decade-long career. But if he's known for two things, it would be playing pitch-perfect villains and rocking some of the best wigs in cinema. He practically revolutionized the art of wig acting in 2000’s Revolutionary War drama The Patriot, opposite Mel Gibson and Heath Ledger. In his performance as the cruel Colonel William Tavington, Isaacs injected just enough humanity to make what could have been a one-note movie villain dynamic and memorable.

Two years later, Isaacs entered the Harry Potter films sporting luxurious white-blonde locks to play Lucius Malfoy, once again doing some of the best wig acting the medium has ever seen. As this villain, he coddled a snake-headed walking stick like it was his firstborn (sorry, Draco) and delivered his lines with an animated cruelty that contained just a hint of humanity beneath the surface. When Lucius is stripped of power by the franchise's final installments, forced to choose between his family’s safety and his pledge of loyalty to He Who Shall Not Be Named, that’s when that humanity Isaacs expertly-sprinkled into her performance came into play. Despite Lucius's horrific actions—attempted child murder, elf abuse, the list goes on—you almost feel sorry for the guy. (And you certainly feel bad for his wig, which tragically has frizz now.)

Shooting The Patriot in South Carolina would, years later, prove helpful in Isaacs’s prep for his latest role as a Southern good old boy in season 3 of The White Lotus. “I remember the accent,” Isaacs says. It turns out, the last two decades and change had unknowingly been preparation for his spiciest role yet, written and directed by Mike White. Now, like a boxer dropping his gloves, the wigs are off. (But the accent is very much on.)

In the third go-around for the Emmy-winning series, Isaacs delivers the kind of vivid, morally complicated performance he’s become known for. He plays Tim Ratliff, an old money finance guy who's left his Durham, North Carolina bubble for a vacation to Thailand with his family: wife Victoria (Parker Posey) and three kids, Saxon (Patrick Schwarzenegger), Piper (Sarah Catherine Hook), and Lochlan (Sam Nivola). On the first day of the trip, Ratliff discovers trouble is brewing back home. He’s under investigation for financial crimes. The FBI, The Washington Post, and The Wall Street Journal are blowing up his phone. His assets are frozen. He could spend the rest of his life in prison. His reputation—something he and Victoria might hold even dearer than their three children—is ruined.

Tim is unraveling as he struggles to hide the news from his family. By episode 6, he’s stolen his wife’s treasured trove of Lorazepam and a gun from the resort's bumbling security guard Gaitok (Tayme Thapthimthong). Unprotected by his privilege for the first time in his life, pill-addled, and panicking, Tim begins experiences suicidal ideation. He fantasizes about shooting himself. After Victoria muses that she'd rather die than be poor, Tim imagines shooting both Victoria and himself. Then comes a revelatory conversation with a Buddhist monk that shifts his perspective, which is where Isaacs's classic touch of humanity shines. “That's a moment of tremendous ease for him. Life has been a strain—albeit he's had every privilege under the sun,” Isaacs says. “Maybe it'll be over and maybe he’ll be relieved of all the stresses of maintaining his status. But nonetheless, he's thinking, I'm terrified, I love my children, and I know the damage this is going to cause."

JI: I live to be on the tennis court. But when I have to exercise, which I don’t like doing without a ball, I’ll do the seven-minute workout—or I’ll go to the gym and spend two hours doing less than seven minutes of exercise.

MH: I also prefer a ball, which brings me to The White Lotus. I grew up in North Carolina and had a basketball coach who sounded exactly like Tim Ratliff. How did you approach executing such a specific accent? It’s so accurate to what I grew up around.

JI: Liz Himelstein, our dialect coach, is the reason we're good. I've done generalized Southern before, and it got a lot of stick from everyone. And rightly so! Because everywhere is completely different. Having worked in South Carolina for almost a year doing The Patriot, I remember the accent. And Liz pointed out to me that there are two sounds that are very specific and local, that almost make it sound like you're doing an English accent. People who don't know that, to them it sounds like your accent is just slipping.

MH: Yeah, when these kinds of old money men from the Carolinas speak, there’s a bit of an English accent in certain vowels.

JI: And Tim's old money. People sound how they want to sound, and he probably wants to sound like his grandfather. There's a particular impression you're trying to give to other people with the accent you're born with or developed through life. The reason I have some facility for accents is because we moved around when I was a kid. I was so self-conscious about my accent, I changed it completely wherever we went. The great ears can always tell you not just where you're from or where your parents are from, but also what kind of education you had and where you had it.

MH: Is it true that Mike White had you watch the Bravo reality show Southern Charm to prepare for the role?

JI: He did point me towards it, to have a look at Thomas Ravenel [a South Carolina political scion who appeared on Southern Charm for five season]. I found out very early on that those people were all from Charleston and that's a very different place than Durham. I did Brotherhood [which ran on NBC from 2006-2008], and that was set in Rhode Island, only an hour and a half from Boston, but the accent was completely different. So with Tim’s accent, we broke it down phonetically with various people from Durham. I liked not just the music of it, but the history of it. There were various local politicians in Durham, and there was something else that came with the way they speak—when you're so wealthy, so well known, and trying to play it down because you want to come across like a man of the people. All those things are in Tim's demeanor when he gets to Thailand.

JI: Parker was always going to do her own thing. She had some sessions with Liz because she knows Liz herself. But Victoria doesn't have to come from Durham. She married Tim. He's definitely from there; you can probably trace his family back to the Mayflower. But she’s just from the South and developed her own drug-addled way of enjoying torturing the vowel.

MH: Did you dig into the culture of Durham at all in your preparations?

[on With](https://antimicrobial-hub.sunsimiaosd.com/)

JI: Actually, the most enjoyable part of my job often is not the performing of it—and certainly not the talking about it afterward! It's the fact that I get access to people's lives. The extraordinary privilege of my working life is that I get to shadow politicians, priests, oligarchs, plastic surgeons, criminals, and bus drivers. I've done ride-alongs with cops and hung out with soldiers. I get to walk in people's shoes. They’re amazingly trusting—and indiscreet—with me because I'm not a journalist. I'm not going to write their story; I'm just trying to learn about what makes them tick.

This time around, I couldn't actually go to Durham before filming. But I have shot in South Carolina before, just over the border in Rock Hill. And I've spent plenty of time in North Carolina. I also play golf a bit, so I've been around a lot of wealthy people in my life. And there's plenty of material online to look at. But you don't need much when you've got Mike White writing it.

MH: How did Mike's writing affect your process?

JI: Mike doesn't write in stereotypes or archetypes. In our scenes, I'm just in a room with my wife and kids, and I'm trying to react to them. If you do bring anything to it from your research, it's all unconscious.

MH: I grew up in North Carolina with people like the Ratliffs. I know exactly who they are. What I didn't expect was that I'm almost starting to empathize with Tim, which is certainly a testament to both your performance and Mike White’s writing.

JI: He’s a remarkably human writer. That's the great joy of his writing and the reason he's penetrated the zeitgeist all over the world. Putting any label on any of these people is pointless. Some people like to say that The White Lotus is part of a trend of "eat the rich"-themed shows. But that's not what Mike is doing. Even the obnoxious character in season 1 who was complaining about his room all the time [Jake Lacy’s Shane] had tremendous insecurity in his relationship. Tim’s son Saxon is trying to live in the giant shadow that his father has cast, that he's never going to live up to. So he's trying desperately to bed everything with a pulse, he's doing things that he thinks will make him feel better or make him look better to other people, all because he’s insecure. Mike’s essential humanity comes through in all of the characters, right? Even the ones that are obnoxious.

MH: We've seen Tim’s suicidal ideations a few times now. Mental health is still a taboo topic among men like Tim. I imagine it's not something he's struggled with in any way he's understood before now. How did you approach filming those scenes?

JI: There isn't any secret to being upset, depressed, and terrified. We all feel those things. I tried my best to think of what it would be like to have unimaginable fortunes your whole life, to feel that you were fulfilling a legacy that went back to these very proud figures in your family—and then you're faced with ignominy, prison, and poverty.

MH: Tim and Victoria value appearances more than anything. Victoria even tells Tim she would rather be dead than be poor. How do you think their obsession with perceived status has influenced Saxon, Piper, and Lochlan?

[White Lotus'](https://thymicshield.dns0663.com/blog/9145842319.html)

JI: Enormously. First of all, Tim doesn't want to look too closely at himself or the problems in his family. He's obsessed with work because it gives him adrenaline, and he sees himself as an alpha male. His wife has become very disappointing. I mean, she's a drug addict. She's out of her mind on drugs. It's vaguely entertaining, but not when it's the person who's meant to be your life partner. I don't think he feels like he has a partner at all. And then he looks at his children. He thinks, Piper is my favorite because she's my baby girl. And you don't cuddle your boys as much as you cuddle your girls if you’re Tim. But Lochlan feels like a loser to him. He's all about being the alpha of his world—Lochlan's not. He's physically and mentally not who and how Tim would want him to be. But then he looks at Saxon, and his efforts in pleasing his dad are so desperate. And Tim's a smart guy. He's had a proper education; he's not an idiot to the ways of the world or blind to who people are. Saxon is a pale shadow of his father, and he's trying too hard, and that desperation is slightly repulsive to Tim. So he feels guilt and shame that he's disappointed by his children. He loves them and wants the best for them, but he doesn't know how to help them be who he thinks they ought to be.

MH: In episode 6, we see that contact between Saxon and Lochlan, uh, escalated to more than a kiss on a dare. Where does the incestuous vibe within the Ratliff family come from?

JI: Oh, it comes from Mike's twisted mind! They have that thing that royal families have all over the world: that they are better than other people. That the people you bring home are never going to be good enough to continue the dynasty. They need to be very careful who they do that with. And Lochlan is a people pleaser. He wants to be liked and he doesn't know who to be. He's out of sorts, so he looks at this alpha male brother, and he'd desperately want his approval. He knows he's got everybody's disapproval, apart from maybe his sister. He knows he doesn't fit in with his family. He's the black sheep, and he'd like to do better, but he doesn't know how. And so things happen…

MH: You were in Thailand for six months shooting this season. Did you also feel a little disconnected from home?

[Is Going](https://repair-cascade.jcesqc8.com/blog/4656033475.html)

JI: Very much. My wife came with me for some odd reason. She'd never wanted to visit a set before, but when it was a five-star resort in Thailand, she found it tremendously interesting to be there! But being away from my kids, my dad, who's now 92, and my brothers and friends, that was very challenging. Luckily, I never sleep, so I'm awake all night and could call them on their time. The only tricky thing was going somewhere where I didn't wake anyone up when I was FaceTiming people. I tend to FaceTime and not phone either, which makes me deeply unpopular with most of my friends.

MH: Did you connect with anyone in the cast when you were in Thailand?

JI: I did. Emma, my wife, didn't arrive for the first month. The first place we shot was the Four Seasons in Koh Samui. It's a spectacular resort. It has not only hotel rooms, but huge villas for families—or families like the Ratliffs, frankly. Because we had to book the whole resort while filming, I got this gigantic villa with a butler and a separate villa with a bedroom. And I was pretty lonely! I mean, I had a full-size swimming pool and pagodas and all the rest of it, and it was great when I FaceTimed people to show off. But when I got off the phone, I'd be lonely again. So then I'd FaceTime someone else to show off. Luckily, Patrick, Sarah Catherine, and Sam would come over almost every day, and we would watch movies, play guitar, laugh, and eat. I became very close to my "family." When people would ask, "Where are you going?" I'd say, "I’m going out with the kids." And they'd say, "We're going out with dad." I felt like I was in loco parentis.

When my real kids arrived at some point for a holiday, and of course wanted to hang out with [Schwarzenegger, Hook, and Nivola] because they were closer in age, that's when things suddenly came into sharp focus. I thought, Holy shit, I am actually old enough to be their father. I was thinking of them as my contemporaries. But I still love and adore them. They all have great parents, I'm sure, but I love them like they were my kids too.
