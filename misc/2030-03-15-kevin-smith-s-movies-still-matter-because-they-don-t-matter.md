# Kevin Smith’s Movies Still Matter (Because They Don’t Matter)

“I’M NOT EVEN supposed to be here today!”

So laments Dante Hicks (Brian O’Halloran) throughout Kevin Smith’s 1994 film Clerks during a particularly arduous shift at the New Jersey convenience store where he works a dead end job. The line is a motto that wouldn’t just become the catchphrase for Clerks’s no-budget slacker ennui, but summarizes the uniquely strange career that Smith has had since making his writing and directing debut in the mid ‘90s. It spawned a run that peaked with the deliciously blasphemous 1999 fantasy Dogma (back in theaters this week) and cratering with… well, it’s better we don’t mention Yoga Hosers.

Over the course of Clerks, Dante freaks out about his girlfriend’s sexual history, destroys his relationship in pursuit of one from the past, and in the film’s lone moment of Kafkaesque injustice, is held responsible for his careless colleague Randal (Jeff Anderson) selling cigarettes to a four-year-old. In essence, what starts as a scrappy ode to blue collar disaffection reveals itself—through vulgar gags, hockey game interstitials, and time-wasting pop culture debates—to be a film about self-sabotaging male neurosis.

Despite featuring some of the most annoying defects of modern pop culture, including a blinkered obsession with the minutiae of nerd fandom and a bro-y comic sensibility that tries to skate past some misogynist and homophobic impulses, Kevin Smith’s slacker classic holds up because of the way his camera regards its self-loathing main characters. When we see two entitled guys who think they deserve better stewing in their own patheticness (encased in the metal shutters of their crappy job, no less), we think, yes, that’s absolutely what being a loser feels like.

Dogma is similarly purposeful in the themes it puts forward. The film, which has spent most of the past 25 years held hostage by Harvey Weinstein, is part Catholic satire, part dark fantasy road trip film, and is just as rewarding and messy as that description implies. It stars then-wunderkids Ben Affleck and Matt Damon, who at the time were fresh off of winning Oscars for Good Will Hunting. The duo play fallen angels Loki and Bartleby, hellbent on reentering heaven, even though doing so would defy the Almighty’s doctrine and cause reality itself to explode. The angel Metatron (the great Alan Rickman) hastily assembles a pilgrimage to thwart the terrible twosome, who later meet more Catholic undesirables played by Linda Fiorentino, Chris Rock and Salma Hayek.

A quarter-century on, the furor whipped up by the Catholic League and evangelicals against the film’s blasphemy seems quaint (Dogma clearly belongs to a pre-Catholic sex abuse scandal world), although Kevin Smith himself joining one of the film’s protests remains very funny. There are plenty of similarities between Smith’s first feature, Clerks, and Dogma, his fourth: the faux-profound, rambling dialogue between jaded characters is the primary source of both humor and theme; the visual style is unsophisticated, most interested in sight gags and letting actors energize the scenes; its most compelling commentary is grounded in pop culture references and the limited insight of slacker wisdom.

But the leaps in Smith’s ambition is clear: Dogma has a dramatic pulse and real stakes, and each character is clearly defined by inventive (if didactic) revisions to Catholic lore. Dogma may sacrifice the scrappy, monochromatic economy of Smith’s debut, but both films feel vibrant and reactive.

Elsewhere, Smith’s early work is more uneven. Mallrats is the ultimate “everything and nothing happens at the local mall” film, with a jarringly polished visual palette and more sex gags made in poor taste than most comedy directors would feel comfortable deploying across an entire career. Chasing Amy may have the most complicated legacy of any Smith film: its male heterosexual wish fulfillment plot centers on a lesbian falling for a handsome, self-insert comic book writer (nearly every Kevin Smith protagonist loves comic books; Smith himself would later pen a ton of comics in addition to his filmmaking career). Amy has driven queer viewers to interrogate how art can provide positive and negative representation of sexual fluidity, all mashed together.

Jay and Silent Bob Strike Back is a shaggy dog road trip following Smith’s most irritating yet charismatic recurring characters: the sex-crazed pot dealer Jay (Jason Mewes) and his (mostly) wordless companion (played by Smith). Strike Back is a scattershot, infantile string of loose Mallrats-like vignettes that successfully skewers big league Hollywood narcissism, but trips up by making the same ugly, regressive jokes as the worst mainstream comedies of the day. (In Dogma, Jay says he and Bob believe in reproductive autonomy, but also hang out at abortion clinics looking to pick up “loose women”; no Kevin Smith film is consistent about whether “being gay” is funny or not).

But the enduring strength of Smith’s shared slacker continuity—the “View Askewniverse,” which connects Clerks, Mallrats, Chasing Amy, Jay and Silent Bob Strike Back, and Dogma, along with four other sequels to Clerks and Jay and Silent Bob—is that the low-stakes, lowbrow comedies are a tonic compared to the mandatory life-or-death stakes of the cinematic universes that audiences recognize today. Rather than insisting that every entry must be important, or that all the connected characters have equally vital but increasingly tedious stuff to do (a staple of the modern Marvel Cinematic Universe), Smith’s experiment in cross-film continuity reminds us just how inconsequential everything we’re watching is. In fact, the View Askewniverse continuity is so loose that an entire film’s worth of insignificant story may have taken place between two other movies and we may not even know—or care.

The recurring appearances of Dante, Randal, Jay and Silent Bob et al. emphasize how valuable it is to be a consistent supporting role in someone else’s life, and that our stories don’t end just because we’re no longer a main character. They also highlight the shared spaces we are drawn to and how uncommon it is for our lives to be majorly and permanently transformed. Smith’s homage to the shared universes of his beloved comic books goes beyond a geek’s desire to make the mundane world around him operate on comic book logic—the connected story concept of his initial films underlines how our lives being totally normal is what makes them special. (Yes, Jay and Silent Bob help save the universe and pull off a Hollywood heist, but it’s not like their Jersey acquaintances give a rat’s ass.)

The 2000s onwards were not kind to Kevin Smith–the public flops Jersey Girl, Zack and Miri Make a Porno, and Cop Out derailed his early momentum. Smith has since turned his focus to outlandish genre projects (if you have any kind of taste for or tolerance of body horror, you need to check out his 2014 film Tusk), podcasts, and comic book presenter roles, as well as making films that incorporate his real-life midlife crisis and health scares. The results are, ahem, mixed, but the fact that Smith’s most compelling work happened decades ago only strengthens their appeal: he was an eager creative whose imperfections have now been encased in ‘90s amber that at least makes them compelling to gawk at and hang out with. The films are vulgar and stupid, but also clumsily sensitive. Their flaws are unique to his voice, which saves them from the fate of the forgotten copycat films that filled the latter '90s years by trying to lift the style of wisecracking Tarantino and gross-out Farrelly Brothers movies.

Kevin Smith is an unlikely artist whose reference-heavy art still feels like it belongs to him alone, earnestly shared with us. Returning to his scrappy, cuss-filled films about nerds and losers trying to make sense of life is as easy and rewarding as returning to a beloved local mall or comic book store; sure, we should have moved on, but then again, why do we feel like we’re supposed to be here today?
