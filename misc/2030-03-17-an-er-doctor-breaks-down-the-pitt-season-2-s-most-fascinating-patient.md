The second season of The Pitt picks up 10 months after the day we saw depicted in season 1, and we very quickly get to see what all of our old friends are up to. Dr. Robby (Noah Wyle) is in for his last day before heading on a three-month sabbatical. Dr. Langdon (Patrick Ball) is back for his first day after getting caught stealing prescription medication and going to rehab, and Nurse Dana (Katherine LaNasa) is back as the charge nurse, helping everyone out and bringing her relentlessly fantastic attitude.

We also quickly get to see what the rest of the doctors are up to. The episode depicts the change-over from the night shift to the day shift (the episode is titled "7:00 AM"), and accordingly, we meet a number of new patients and cases. While there's lots of injuries, emergencies, and viscerally believable imagery of surgery and internal organs, the most interesting new case and patient has been assigned to Dr. McKay (Fiona Dourif).

[Most Fascinating Patient](https://musclemend.sunsimiaosd.com/)

Michael Williams seems mostly OK. He's sitting in his hospital gown, and tells Dr. McKay that he fell over a laundry basket in the middle of the night, and hurt his wrist. She asks if he's hit his head, and he responds somewhat defensively, asking if that's listed in his chart. He's snippy and clearly doesn't really want to be there, making it clear he also wants to get in and out of the hospital as quickly as possible, adding that he wants to "keep the bullshit extra charges to a minimum," something that clearly annoys McKay.

She does her best to help him, while he clearly just wants to get out of there. She wants to get an X-Ray on his wrist—which he says he fell on—but he insists he's OK and can go home. While this very clearly goes against her better judgement (Dourif is so good at showing the dissonance between McKay's strong personality and her strong instincts as a medical professional), she allows him to go... until they shake hands on the way out and his wrist is still clearly in pain. Mr. Williams agrees to the X-Ray. Throughout, she also seems a bit nervous with some of Mr. Williams's more aggressive and erratic behavior.

The biggest medical mystery, though, comes near the very end of the episode, when Dr. McKay returns to Mr. Williams's room one final time. He wants to go home, but something is very, very, wrong—when Dr. McKay checks his leg, it kicks, unclear to us whether it's in pain or reaction to McKay going near it. He's confused by the wrist splint she brought, and she signals for help from a nurse, worried about possible violence or aggressive behavior. When McKay asks whether he knows what day it is ("Taco Tuesday") or if he knows where he is, he can't give straight, accurate answers. The Pitt will let us know what's going on with Mr. Williams as the season progresses, but it's clear that there's more going on than just a hurt wrist at this point.

A Doctor's Opinion on Mr. Williams and Dr. McKay in The Pitt

[Doctor Breaks](https://3899241.com/8ro9i65/)

For an expert's opinion, we reached out to Dr. Robert Glatter, the Assistant Professor of Emergency Medicine at Lenox Hill Hospital, an experienced ER doctor, and a member of the MH Advisory Board. Dr. Glatter reviewed the scenes between Dr. McKay and Mr. Williams, and shared his own take with Men's Health.

"He appears agitated and evasive when questioned, but is only alert to person, not place or time," he explained. "Dr. McKay is absolutely justified in her concern about a potential head injury such as an intracranial hemorrhage or even skull fracture. The patient has 3 distracting painful injuries—wrist, knee and scalp pain--which can delay diagnosis of a potentially life threatening head injury."

Dr. Glatter also draws attention to Mr. Williams's comments on how he self-treats, and Dr. McKay's concern for potential violence from her patient.

"The patient also makes a statement about how a scotch and a Vicodin might be the most effective treatment for his pain, making substance abuse another factor contributing to his symptoms, presenting as opiate or alcohol withdrawal as considerations for his erratic and aggressive behaviors," Dr. Glatter explains. "During the encounter, Dr. McKay appears uncomfortable in his presence, possibly related to the threat of violence against her, as she asks for another staff member to stand guard outside the patient’s room."

Dr. Glatter commends The Pitt for depicting a realistic—and nuanced—portrayal of an ER patient. "This is common type of patient we see in the ED, with multiple conditions posing a threat to himself and the staff," he says.
