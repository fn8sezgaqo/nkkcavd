The cancer is likely caused by plantar pressure—the pressure put on your feet simply from standing—and shear stress, which is the friction applied to your feet when walking or running, Dr. Okuyama explains.

That pressure and friction may damage your cells in ways similar to ultraviolet rays, though it’s unclear if they do so in exactly the same way, he adds.

But don’t hang up your sneakers just yet: More research needs to be done to determine whether simply spending more time on your feet raises your risk.

Plus, melanoma on your soles is really rare. For one, melanoma in general accounts for less than one percent of all skin cancer diagnoses, according to 2016 data from the American Cancer Society (ACS).

And in Caucasians—the racial group most likely to develop melanoma, with a 2.5 percent lifetime risk, according to the ACS—only about 3 percent of melanoma cases occur on the feet, Dr. Okuyama estimates.

The percentage is higher in other racial groups, though—but their chances of developing melanoma overall are much lower, too.

For instance, up to one third of all melanomas develop on the feet for Asian Americans, and it’s one of the most common locations for African Americans, says Dr. Okuyama.

What’s more melanoma on your soles is rare in young guys overall: Your risk grows as you age, because your cells weaken and become more vulnerable to damage, Dr. Okuyama says.

So you definitely don’t need to change how you walk, or how often you walk.

Instead, just make sure you check the soles of your feet once a year to look for anything suspicious, he says.
