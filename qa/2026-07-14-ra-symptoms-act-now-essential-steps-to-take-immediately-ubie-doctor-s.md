Experiencing rheumatoid arthritis symptoms? Here's what to do

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune condition that causes inflammation in your joints. Early recognition and action can slow its progression, ease discomfort and preserve joint function. If you're noticing unusual joint aches, stiffness or swelling, follow these steps based on current guidelines (Aletaha et al. 2010; Smolen et al. 2014) and trusted medical sources.

Common rheumatoid arthritis symptoms

Joint pain and tenderness, often worst in small joints (fingers, wrists)

Morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes

Swelling, warmth or redness around affected joints

Symptoms on both sides of the body (symmetrical pattern)

Fatigue, low-grade fever or general malaise

Loss of grip strength or difficulty performing daily tasks

Step 1: Track what you feelKeep a simple daily log of your symptoms:

Time of day when pain or stiffness peaks

Which joints are affected

Duration of stiffness and pain intensity (scale of 1–10)

Any triggers (activity, weather changes, stress)

Impact on sleep, mood and daily tasks

A clear diary helps you and your doctor spot patterns, measure changes over time and choose the best treatment.

Step 3: Schedule a healthcare visitIf your symptoms persist for more than a few weeks, book an appointment with your primary care provider or a rheumatologist. Be ready to share:

Your symptom diary

Family history of autoimmune or inflammatory conditions

Any recent infections or medications you're taking

Early referral to a rheumatologist improves outcomes, since treatment is most effective when started promptly.

Step 4: Understand the classification criteriaDoctors use the 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria (Aletaha et al. 2010) to help confirm an RA diagnosis. You're scored on:

Joint involvement (number and size of joints affected)

Blood tests for specific antibodies (RF, anti-CCP)

Inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR)

Duration of symptoms (more or less than 6 weeks)

A total score of 6 or more (out of 10) supports an RA diagnosis. Knowing these criteria helps you follow your doctor's reasoning.

Step 5: Expect key diagnostic testsTo confirm RA and rule out other causes, your doctor may order:

Blood tests: RF (rheumatoid factor), anti-CCP (anti–cyclic citrullinated peptide), ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate), CRP (C-reactive protein)

Imaging: X-rays to check joint damage; ultrasound or MRI to detect early inflammation

These tests help gauge disease activity and guide treatment choices based on the 2013 EULAR management update (Smolen et al. 2014).

Step 6: Start treatment earlyOnce RA is diagnosed, early treatment prevents joint damage and disability. Typical approaches include:

Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) such as methotrexate—these slow the disease process

Short-term NSAIDs (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) or corticosteroids to control pain and swelling

Biologic agents (e.g., TNF inhibitors) for moderate to severe disease or if initial DMARDs aren't enough

Regular labs to monitor blood counts, liver and kidney function

Following a "treat-to-target" plan—adjusting medications to reach low disease activity or remission—is now standard care.

Step 7: Monitor your progressRA is a lifelong condition for many people. Regular check-ups every 3–6 months help ensure your treatment is on track. Your doctor will:

Measure disease activity (joint counts, patient questionnaires)

Repeat blood tests (ESR, CRP, liver enzymes)

Adjust medications based on response and side effects

If you're on methotrexate or other DMARDs, your doctor may also assess liver stiffness. Newer guidelines suggest noninvasive measures (e.g., FibroScan®) if there's concern about drug-related liver injury (Tsochatzis et al. 2014).

Step 8: Adopt helpful lifestyle changesAlongside medical treatment, simple habits can ease symptoms:

Gentle, regular exercise (walking, swimming, yoga) to keep joints flexible

Balanced diet rich in omega-3s (fatty fish, flaxseeds), antioxidants (fruits, vegetables) and lean proteins

When to seek urgent careSome RA symptoms or complications need immediate attention:

High fever, severe fatigue or unexplained weight loss

Signs of serious infection (e.g., mouth sores, persistent cough, burning with urination) while on immunosuppressive drugs

Sudden, severe joint pain with trauma

Chest pain or shortness of breath

Always speak to a doctor or call emergency services if you have life-threatening or rapidly worsening problems.
