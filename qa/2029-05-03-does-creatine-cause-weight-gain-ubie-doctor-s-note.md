# Does creatine cause weight gain? | Ubie Doctor's Note

Does creatine cause weight gain?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Does creatine cause weight gain? Yes—but it's usually water weight, not fat. Most people gain 1–2 kg (2–4 lbs) in the first week as creatine pulls water into the muscles, especially during a loading phase. Any later gains from consistent training typically reflect added lean muscle, and weight stabilizes once you switch to a maintenance dose of 3–5 g/day.

How to manage creatine-related weight gain:

Skip the loading phase to minimize early water retention—results take longer, but gains are more gradual.

Stay well-hydrated to support muscle function and reduce bloating.

Avoid creatine if you have kidney or liver disease, or take medications affecting kidney function—consult your doctor first.

Not all weight changes from creatine are normal, though. Sudden bloating, swelling, fatigue, or rapid weight gain can sometimes signal an underlying issue unrelated to your supplement. Does Creatine Cause Weight Gain?

Creatine is one of the most studied and widely used supplements in sports nutrition. Athletes, fitness enthusiasts, and even weekend warriors turn to creatine for its well-documented benefits: improved strength, enhanced power output, and better exercise performance. A common question that comes up, however, is: Does creatine cause weight gain?

This guide explores how creatine affects body weight, why you might see changes on the scale, and what the science says. We'll also cover key safety considerations and when to seek professional medical advice.

How Creatine Works and Why Weight Change Happens

At its core, creatine is a compound naturally produced by the body and stored in skeletal muscle. It helps regenerate adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the energy currency used during high-intensity exercise.

Weight changes with creatine supplementation primarily stem from two factors:

Water Retention in Muscles

Creatine draws water into muscle cells (cell volumization).

This intracellular water boost can add 1–2 kilograms (2–4 pounds) of fluid weight during the initial "loading phase."

Increased Muscle Mass

Over weeks of training with creatine, you may build more lean muscle.

Muscle tissue weighs more than fat tissue by volume.

As strength and workout intensity improve, muscle gains can translate to a gradual rise in body weight.

What the Research Says

1. Short-Term Water Weight (Rawson & Volek, 2003)

Rawson and Volek reviewed creatine supplementation combined with resistance training. Key takeaways:

A typical loading phase (20 g/day for 5–7 days) led to an average weight gain of 1–2 kg.

Much of this gain was due to water moving into muscle cells.

Once you switch to a maintenance dose (3–5 g/day), weight tends to stabilize.

2. Long-Term Muscle Gains (Buford et al., 2007)

The International Society of Sports Nutrition's position stand outlined:

Creatine users often see greater strength and muscle mass increases over 4–12 weeks compared to placebo.

Weight gain after the initial loading phase is more likely from additional muscle rather than fat.

Proper resistance training and nutrition are crucial to direct these gains toward muscle growth.

3. Safety in Healthy Adults

Neither study found evidence that creatine supplementation leads to fat gain or causes harmful fluid buildup in healthy individuals.

Creatine has a strong safety profile when taken at recommended doses (3–5 g/day maintenance).

Health Considerations and Special Populations

For most people, creatine is safe. However, certain conditions warrant extra caution or medical oversight:

Kidney or Liver Issues

Healthy kidneys and liver handle normal creatine intake well.

If you have existing kidney disease or liver cirrhosis (Tsochatzis, Bosch & Burroughs, 2014), consult your doctor before starting creatine.

Medication Interactions

Some medications (e.g., nephrotoxic drugs) may interact with high creatine loads.

Always discuss supplements with your healthcare provider if you're on prescription medicine.

Hydration Status

Drinking adequate water helps manage the temporary fluid shift into muscles.

Aim for at least 2–3 liters of water per day, more if you sweat heavily during workouts.

Tips to Manage and Understand Weight Gain

If you're thinking about using creatine but worried about the scale, consider these practical strategies:

Skip the Loading Phase

Instead of 20 g/day for 5–7 days, take a steady 3–5 g/day from the start.

This approach minimizes rapid water retention while still boosting muscle creatine stores—though it takes a bit longer to saturate muscles.

Track Body Composition, Not Just Scale Weight

Use measurements like body fat percentage, tape measurements, or how your clothes fit to gauge true muscle gains.

Pair with Resistance Training

Creatine works best when combined with a structured strength program.

Without proper training, extra water weight won't translate into lasting muscle improvements.

Monitor Hydration and Electrolytes

Ensure you're consuming enough fluids and electrolytes to support the increased water in muscle cells.

When to Seek Help

Most creatine-related weight changes are benign and expected. However, if you experience unusual symptoms while taking creatine—such as persistent fatigue, changes in urination, or unexplained discomfort—you can use a free symptom checker to get personalized health information and guidance on whether you should consult a medical professional.

Always remember, if you have serious or life-threatening symptoms, speak to a doctor right away.

Speak to Your Healthcare Provider

While creatine is one of the safest and most effective supplements available, individual health factors vary. Before beginning any supplement regimen—especially if you have known medical conditions or take prescription medications—please speak to a doctor. They can provide personalized advice, run necessary lab tests, and help you use creatine wisely and safely.

Bottom Line

Creatine supplementation often leads to an initial, modest weight gain from water retention.

Over weeks of consistent training and maintenance dosing, additional weight gain is typically due to increased muscle mass, not fat.

Creatine is safe for healthy adults at recommended doses, but those with kidney or liver issues should get medical clearance first.

Monitoring body composition, staying well-hydrated, and talking to a doctor will help you optimize creatine's benefits without unwelcome surprises on the scale.
