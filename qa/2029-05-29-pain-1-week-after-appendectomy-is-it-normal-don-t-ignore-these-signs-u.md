Is It Normal to Still Have Pain 1 Week After Appendectomy?

Recovering from an appendectomy usually involves some discomfort. If you're experiencing 1 week after appendix surgery pain, you're not alone. Pain levels vary from person to person, but understanding what's typical versus what's a warning sign can help you feel more in control of your recovery.

Most people notice a steady improvement in pain over the first week after surgery. Here's a general guide:

Days 1–3: Moderate to severe pain in the abdomen, especially around the incision(s).

Days 4–7: Pain shifts to mild or moderate, often described as soreness or achiness.

After Day 7: Many feel only occasional twinges or discomfort, especially with movement.

If you still have pain 1 week after appendix surgery, it may simply be your body's way of continuing healing. Tissues, muscles and nerve endings take time to settle down.

Common Causes of Pain at One Week

Incision Healing• Tenderness around the surgical site• Occasional sharp sensations as skin and muscles repair

Internal Swelling• Inflammation of tissues where the appendix was removed• Fluid collection (small seromas) that usually shrink on their own

Gas and Bloating• Carbon dioxide used during laparoscopic surgery can irritate the diaphragm• Referred pain felt in the shoulder or upper abdomen

Scar Tissue Formation• Mild discomfort as new collagen forms• Stiffness when stretching or bending

Muscle Strain• Abdominal muscles regain strength gradually• Overdoing chores or lifting can prolong soreness

When Pain Is Still Normal at One Week

You may consider your pain within normal limits if it matches one or more of the following:

Dull, aching soreness around the incision

Mild discomfort when standing up, coughing or laughing

Sporadic sharp pokes that quickly fade

Low-grade cramping or tugging sensations

Pain that improves with rest or over-the-counter pain relievers

Warning Signs: When to Get Medical Help

While some discomfort is expected, certain red flags mean you should seek medical advice promptly:

Persistent or worsening pain despite medication

Fever over 100.4°F (38°C) or chills

Increased redness, swelling or warmth at the incision site

Pus-like discharge or foul odor from the wound

Nausea, vomiting or inability to pass gas/bowel movements

Rapid heart rate, dizziness or feeling very weak

If you experience any of the above, talk to your doctor or head to the nearest emergency department. These could signal infection, bleeding or a bowel obstruction.

Tips to Manage 1 Week After Appendix Surgery Pain

Take prescribed painkillers on schedule for the first few days.

Transition to acetaminophen or ibuprofen as directed.

Short, slow walks around the house or yard help reduce stiffness.

Factors That May Prolong Pain

Type of Surgery: Open appendectomy (larger incision) often means more soreness compared to laparoscopic methods.

Complicated Appendicitis: If your appendix had ruptured or caused an abscess, recovery can be slower.

Individual Health: Smokers, diabetics or people with certain chronic illnesses may heal more slowly.

Infection or Bleeding: Any postoperative complication can extend discomfort.

When to Call Your Surgeon

Contact your surgical team if:

Pain is out of proportion to what you expect

You cannot tolerate oral medications

You see signs of wound infection

You develop digestive issues that worsen over time

Your surgeon knows your case best and can recommend tests (bloodwork, ultrasound, CT scan) or a follow-up visit.

Final Thoughts

Experiencing some level of 1 week after appendix surgery pain is completely normal as your body heals. Mild to moderate soreness, occasional sharp twinges and slight swelling are part of the process. By following your doctor's instructions, staying active within limits and caring for your incision, you'll continue to make progress.

However, always trust your instincts. If something feels seriously wrong—especially if you see signs of infection, high fever or unbearable pain—don't hesitate to speak to a doctor or visit the emergency department. Your health and safety come first.
